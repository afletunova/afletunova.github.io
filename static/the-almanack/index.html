Google’s Gemini steps out of the chat box and into the Mac desktop

Google is turning Gemini into something more ambitious than a conversational assistant: a tool that can operate Mac apps, websites and local files on its own while you continue working. With a new “Computer Use” feature, the model can click, type and navigate across a desktop workflow in the background, marking a notable shift from AI that answers questions to AI that performs tasks.

The Discovery/Development

Google has added a new “Computer Use” feature to the Gemini Mac app, allowing the assistant to complete multistep desktop tasks across local files, desktop applications and websites, according to reporting from The Decoder, which cites Google’s own announcement. Rather than requiring the user to stay in front of the action, the feature runs in an isolated background window with a live chat preview, so the screen and cursor remain free for other work.

Google says the system is designed around explicit user control: people choose which apps Gemini can access, can watch each step live, may pause at any time and are asked to approve key actions beforehand. The company’s examples include batch-editing photos and 3D scenes, filling out shipping forms using local PDFs and building presentations. The feature is rolling out in English in the US, and the Gemini Mac app is available to download, according to Google’s announcement as cited by The Decoder.

Why This Matters

The significance of this update is not just that Gemini can now do more work, but that it is being positioned as a persistent desktop agent rather than a chat interface. That is an important product shift because it moves AI deeper into the everyday software stack: files, menus, forms and browser tabs. For users, that could mean less repetitive clicking and less context-switching between apps. For Google, it is another step towards making Gemini an operating-layer for digital work rather than merely a box in which questions are typed and answers are returned.

The move also places Google more directly into a competitive race. The Decoder notes that OpenAI has “also focused on Computer Use recently,” a reminder that desktop automation is becoming a priority across the frontier AI sector. The competition is no longer only about who can answer fastest or write best, but who can reliably act inside a computer environment on behalf of the user.

How It Works

At a practical level, the feature translates a user’s goal into a sequence of UI actions. Google says Gemini can “click, type, and navigate menus and forms,” which suggests a model that observes the interface, decides on a next step and then executes it just as a person would. The key technical distinction is that the model is not simply generating text about what to do; it is taking over the procedural work of operating software.

Google’s design choices point to an attempt to reduce the obvious risks of letting an AI loose on a desktop. The isolated background window keeps the task running without interfering with the user’s current workspace. The live chat preview gives the person visibility into what the agent is doing. And the ability to approve important actions beforehand indicates a permission model intended to slow down or interrupt potentially consequential steps. In other words, the system is framed less as an autonomous free agent than as a supervised digital assistant with scoped access.

The examples Google chose are telling. Batch editing images or 3D scenes, extracting details from PDFs to complete shipping forms, and assembling presentations are all chores that share a common pattern: they are repetitive, often involve copying information between applications, and can consume significant time despite being relatively simple in concept. That is precisely the kind of work AI agent systems are now targeting.

Implications and Future Directions

If the feature works well in practice, its clearest consequence is convenience. A user could set Gemini to handle an administrative task and keep working elsewhere while the job progresses in the background. That is a meaningful change in how people might interact with software: the computer becomes less of a destination for direct manipulation and more of a workspace shared with an automated operator.

But the shift also raises questions that are built into any system with desktop access. The more an AI can interact with files, apps and websites, the more important it becomes to define exactly where it can operate, what it can see and when it must ask permission. Google’s emphasis on user-chosen app access and pre-approved key actions suggests the company recognises that a helpful agent can also become a source of mistakes if its scope is too broad.

Expert Perspectives

The source material does not quote outside experts, but it does make clear that Google is framing the feature as a controlled form of automation rather than fully open-ended autonomy. The company’s emphasis on visibility, pausing and approval reflects an awareness that trust is central to adoption. In this area, the product’s success will depend not only on capability, but on whether users feel they can safely delegate work to it.

The Bigger Picture

Gemini’s new Mac feature fits a wider trend in AI: the push to make models ambient, embedded and operational rather than merely conversational. In that sense, the development is relevant to AI safety as much as to productivity. Once a model can move through files, websites and desktop tools, issues of permission, oversight, mistaken actions and unintended side effects become far more concrete than in a text-only chat.

The broader race is therefore about more than intelligence scores or benchmark gains. It is about which company can build an AI system that people will actually allow into the flow of daily work. Google’s Computer Use feature suggests one answer: give the model enough agency to be useful, but wrap it in user controls strong enough to make that agency feel manageable. Whether that balance proves durable will help determine how quickly AI becomes not just a tool we ask, but a system we trust to act.

Princeton neuroscientist’s detention throws a stark light on immigration, research and scientific fragility

A Princeton graduate student whose work spans theory, computation and experiment was detained by U.S. immigration officials at an airport in Washington, D.C., just after presenting at the Society for Neuroscience meeting. The case of Eva Yi Xie has jolted her lab, raised legal questions about her status, and underscored how quickly a single immigration action can disrupt years of scientific training and ongoing research.

The Discovery/Development

Eva Yi Xie, a third-year graduate student in Carlos Brody’s lab at Princeton University, was detained yesterday by U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement while travelling from the Society for Neuroscience annual meeting in Washington, D.C., to California, Brody said. Xie had just presented a poster on the cognitive functions of the entorhinal cortex at the meeting. Brody described the detention as “an absolute shock” and said her science had been “grind[ing] … to a screeching halt.”

Xie is not a peripheral figure in the lab. Brody said she is “brilliant and incredibly talented,” and co-adviser Nathaniel Daw described her as “incredibly broad and ambitious.” Her work centres on the science of decision-making, combining theoretical, computational and experimental neuroscience. As an undergraduate in Ila Fiete’s lab at MIT, she developed a model of how the hippocampus and entorhinal cortex make decisions; Brody said later experimental work in his lab suggested that model was correct. Xie also studies perceptual discrimination in rodents using both theoretical and experimental techniques.

Why This Matters

The immediate concern is personal: Xie is reportedly being held in a temporary processing and holding facility in Chantilly, Virginia, which Brody said lacks beds, showers and the infrastructure to feed detainees. But the wider significance reaches far beyond one scientist. Her detention has interrupted a research programme at a major U.S. university and exposed how dependent American science remains on international talent at every stage, from undergraduate training to doctoral research.

Brody said Xie immigrated to the United States from China in 2016. Her visa had expired, but her asylum application allowed her to remain in the country legally, her lawyers told The New York Times. Her lawyers have challenged the legality of her detention, according to court records. That combination of facts — a high-achieving researcher, a pending legal challenge, and a detention that appears to have caught colleagues off guard — makes the case a warning signal for universities that rely on mobile, international scientific labour.

How It Works

Xie’s research sits in a part of neuroscience where theory and experiment constantly inform one another. Brody said she combines theoretical, computational and experimental approaches to understand decision-making. In practical terms, that means using mathematical models and neural recordings or behavioural experiments to ask how the brain weighs evidence, represents space and turns information into choice.

Brody pointed to work Xie did with the hippocampus and entorhinal cortex — brain regions long linked to memory and navigation — as an example of this approach. Her model suggested that these regions carry a joint representation of cognitive evidence and physical space. Later experimental work in Brody’s lab supported that idea, he said. That matters because these areas are central to understanding how brains organise experience, and because the same mix of modelling and data is increasingly common across neuroscience, including in areas that intersect with artificial intelligence.

Implications and Future Directions

The first consequence is simple and immediate: the research stops, at least for now. Daw said Xie’s detention was “incredibly disruptive” and that he expected she would have been back at work within days. In a field where graduate students often carry much of the day-to-day intellectual and experimental load, the removal of one researcher can stall ongoing projects, delay papers and slow the training of undergraduates and junior lab members who depend on that person’s expertise.

There is also a broader institutional effect. Princeton has arranged legal representation for Xie, according to university spokesperson Michael Hotchkiss, though the university did not respond to The Transmitter’s request for comment. ICE and the Department of Homeland Security also did not respond to emailed requests for comment. Those silences leave open crucial questions about why Xie was detained, how the legal process will unfold and whether universities have any meaningful warning when such actions occur.

Expert Perspectives

Brody’s reaction was both emotional and political. “My first reaction was shock and pain, and horror and disbelief that this could be happening,” he said. He added that Xie is “a gentle, kind, caring person who is contributing enormously to the United States” and insisted, “She belongs back home in Princeton doing the science that she loves.”

He was even more emphatic in defending her place in the community: “She is one of us. She is one of the best of us. And this shouldn’t be happening to any of us.” Yanar similarly described Xie as someone who “came already knowing how to hit the ground running,” while Daw said her detention was “incredibly disruptive” to her work. Taken together, those comments frame the case not as an abstract policy dispute, but as the sudden removal of a trusted colleague, mentor and collaborator.

The Bigger Picture

This case sits at the intersection of neuroscience and the increasingly visible politics of scientific mobility. Princeton’s neuroscience programme, like many elite research centres, depends on students and postdoctoral researchers arriving from abroad with specialised skills, fresh ideas and the willingness to tackle hard problems. Xie’s work — bridging theory, computation and experiment in decision-making — reflects the kind of interdisciplinary research that is often most vulnerable to administrative interruption, because it relies on continuity, collaboration and long training periods.

There is also a deeper warning here for the broader science ecosystem. Neuroscience, and especially computational neuroscience, is becoming more integrated with data-driven methods and with the broader conversation about intelligence, including AI. When a promising researcher is removed from the system abruptly, the loss is not only personal. It weakens the pipeline of expertise that supports future discoveries, teaching and cross-disciplinary work. The case of Eva Yi Xie shows how immigration enforcement can become a scientific issue — not in the abstract, but at the level of a single lab, a single project and a single career.
