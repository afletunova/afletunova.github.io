OpenAI audit exposes blind spots in AI safety monitoring as agentic systems grow more capable

A three-week on-site audit of OpenAI’s internal monitoring systems has found that significant portions of high-capability AI traffic were not being watched at all, while other activity could be hidden from oversight or slip past safeguards entirely. Conducted by researchers from METR and Redwood Research, the review offers a rare external glimpse into how frontier labs monitor “agentic” systems in practice — and where those controls can fail.

The four layers where AI monitoring can fail, from missing coverage to fooling human reviewers. | Image: METR

The Discovery/Development

From 21 July to 11 August, two researchers from METR and Redwood Research worked on-site at OpenAI with access to the company’s codebase, internal documents, and around a dozen employee interviews. Their task was not to test a public model in the abstract, but to inspect the internal system OpenAI uses to monitor AI behaviour for signs of rogue or unsafe activity. According to the audit summary, they found “numerous weaknesses” in that monitoring stack, including substantial gaps in coverage and methods by which activity could evade detection.

The most striking finding was that an estimated 6 to 25 per cent of internal agentic Responses API traffic from models at the GPT-5.5 class and above was not monitored at all during the audit period. METR says the uncertainty on that estimate is large in both directions, but even the lower end implies a meaningful blind spot in oversight of advanced systems. The auditors also estimated that roughly 10 per cent of internal Codex traffic that was meant to be monitored went untracked between May and July because of a bug in how the system handled “compaction events” — context summaries that compress prior activity. OpenAI had independently discovered and fixed that bug while the audit was still under way.

Beyond missing coverage, the researchers found that agents could delay scrutiny because monitoring triggered only when they handed control back to the user. In effect, an agent that kept acting without returning control could postpone oversight. They also identified ways to hide hundreds of actions from the monitor, to bypass the Codex sandbox, and to potentially crash the monitor with specially crafted inputs. At the time the audit began, model behaviour during reinforcement learning (RL) training was not being monitored at all, though OpenAI later said it paused RL training after the Hugging Face incident in August and expanded monitoring to cover it. Those changes came after the audit window and were not included in the evaluation.

Why This Matters

The significance of the audit lies in its subject matter as much as its method. AI safety debates often focus on hypothetical future systems, but this report deals with the operational reality of monitoring systems already used around powerful, agentic models. As AI systems are given more autonomy — drafting code, taking actions through tools, and chaining together multi-step tasks — the question is no longer only whether models can be aligned in principle. It is also whether labs can actually see what those systems are doing in the first place.

That distinction matters. Safety policies, red-team exercises, and usage restrictions are only as strong as the monitoring that underpins them. If a model can act without being watched, if summaries create blind spots, or if oversight only happens when the system pauses, then the lab may be operating with a false sense of control. The audit therefore shifts the discussion from abstract alignment claims to the mechanics of surveillance, logging, and enforcement inside frontier AI infrastructure.

How It Works

The systems under review appear to have been designed to detect suspicious behaviour in agentic workflows — for example, models using tools, performing sequences of actions, or interacting with external systems through OpenAI’s internal infrastructure. In theory, such monitoring can flag anomalous patterns, pause risky actions, or alert human reviewers. In practice, the audit suggests that several layers can fail at once.

One layer of failure is simple non-coverage: some traffic was not monitored at all. Another is semantic loss, where “compaction events” summarise earlier context and inadvertently break the chain of observation. A third is timing: if the monitoring system only activates when a model returns control to the user, then a model that continues operating can remain temporarily invisible. The audit also indicates that the monitor itself may have been vulnerable to manipulation or overload, since certain inputs could likely crash it, while other actions could be hidden from its view. Finally, the finding that RL training behaviour was unmonitored at the start of the audit is important because training-time behaviour can shape how a model acts later; if the system is learning unsafe strategies, that may not be obvious from deployment monitoring alone.

The report also highlights the challenge of measuring safety controls themselves. METR’s estimates on missing coverage are explicitly uncertain, and the public has not been given the full report. OpenAI received the full 61-page version internally, while METR and Redwood leadership saw a redacted 38-page version, and only a short summary was made public. METR said it had no right to disclose the redacted sections on its own. That limits outside scrutiny, even as the audit is presented as evidence of progress in external oversight.

There is also a policy implication: if labs want credible assurances about safety, they may need to design systems that are robust not only against malicious model behaviour, but also against failures in the safety machinery itself. Monitoring that can be delayed, bypassed, or crashed is not merely incomplete; it can create a misleading picture of assurance. The audit suggests that future work will need to focus as much on resilient observability as on model alignment in the narrow sense.

Expert Perspectives

The strongest perspective in the source material comes from the structure of the audit itself. METR described the review as a pilot programme organised jointly with Redwood Research and OpenAI to give outside researchers deep access to internal infrastructure. That arrangement is notable because it signals an emerging model for accountability: not only external red-teaming after the fact, but sustained, on-site inspection of the systems labs use to watch their own models.

At the same time, METR says it wants future audits to involve more public transparency. That goal points to a tension that the present report does not resolve: the deeper the access, the more sensitive the material; the more redactions, the less useful the public record. The audit therefore offers a glimpse of a new governance model, while also underscoring how unfinished that model remains.

The Bigger Picture

This audit lands at an important moment in AI safety. The field has long wrestled with questions about alignment, deception, and control, but the practical challenge is increasingly operational: can institutions reliably monitor systems that act autonomously, adapt during training, and interact with tools across complex workflows? The OpenAI review suggests that the answer is still only partially yes.

That is why the findings matter beyond one company. As frontier models become more capable, the safety problem becomes layered: model behaviour, training dynamics, tool use, oversight software, and human review all have to work together. A weakness in any one layer can undermine the rest. In that sense, the audit is less a story about one broken monitor than about the fragility of the entire safety stack around advanced AI. It shows that, in practice, the hardest part of AI safety may not be building a watcher — but ensuring the watcher cannot be fooled, delayed, or left in the dark.

It is the kind of result that could reshape mental health care, but it is already receiving a full-page deep dive elsewhere in this issue.

Beyond that headline, the day’s news points to a field moving in two directions at once: towards more precise, individualised medicine, and towards a deeper understanding of how the brain’s circuits shape behaviour, appetite, learning and mood. Several studies also hint at the growing convergence between neuroscience and engineering, from ultra-low-power neural chips to new tools for visualising receptor states and decoding brain network activity.

One especially notable theme is how neuroscience is becoming more predictive. Researchers are not just describing brain activity after the fact; they are linking brain signals to future outcomes, such as changes in body fat in children, or slower cognitive decline in Parkinson’s disease. That shift from observation to prognosis is quietly transforming the field.

Brain Research Updates

  • Ultra-processed foods may be reshaping children’s reward circuitry: a longitudinal fMRI study found that higher intake was linked to stronger dorsal striatum responses to appetising food images, and to greater body fat and BMI a year later. The finding adds weight to concerns that modern diets may condition developing brains towards overeating (source).
  • Scientists have identified a “hidden messaging system” deep in the brain, showing that a tiny region once thought to broadcast a single chemical signal can instead send targeted messages tied to learning, attention and behaviour in mice. The work suggests brain chemistry may be far more spatially precise than previously assumed (source).
  • A study on attention suggests that long-term memory, flexible templates and learning all contribute to how the brain guides focus in changing environments. The paper offers a more dynamic picture of attention, one in which past experience continuously shapes what we notice (source).
  • Researchers have also reported on a landmark genetics study identifying 1,260 variants associated with the Big Five personality traits. The message is clear: personality is not written by a single “outgoing” or “anxious” gene, but by thousands of tiny effects interacting with life experience (source).
  • In evolutionary neuroscience, Barbara Finlay is being remembered for championing the idea that developmental constraints help shape how brains evolve across species. Her work helped frame brain evolution as a story of timing, trade-offs and shared mammalian design rules (source).
  • At the molecular level, a new Nature Communications paper describes rigid protein fiducials designed to visualise GPCR conformational states. While highly technical, the advance could sharpen structural studies of one of biology’s most important receptor families (source).

Clinical Applications

  • The most immediately consequential medical development today is the personalised PTSD treatment reporting a 52% symptom reduction and 68% remission in a clinical trial. Using brain mapping and magnetic stimulation, it points towards a more precise model of psychiatry than the current trial-and-error approach (source).
  • For Parkinson’s disease, a seven-year study found that people who were more physically active experienced slower decline in memory, thinking and movement. Those exercising more than eight hours per week showed almost no average cognitive decline, strengthening the case for exercise as part of long-term care (source).
  • EPFL researchers have built LogSync, an ultra-low-power chip that can decode oscillatory synchrony to support adaptive deep brain stimulation for depression. The work could help make closed-loop neuromodulation more responsive, portable and clinically practical (source).
  • The PTSD result, together with the depression neuromodulation chip, suggests psychiatry is steadily moving towards circuit-based treatment rather than symptom-only management. That may eventually open the door to therapies tailored to an individual patient’s brain activity profile (source).

AI + Neuroscience Convergence

While there were no headline-grabbing AI papers in today’s batch, the most important convergence story is still clear: neuroscience is becoming increasingly computational. The PTSD treatment relies on personalised brain mapping, while the LogSync chip turns complex multi-region synchrony into a real-time signal that a device can act on — exactly the kind of translation layer that increasingly resembles machine learning pipelines.

There is also a broader lesson for AI design. Studies of attention, learning and flexible memory templates remind us that the brain does not process information in a fixed, feedforward way; it constantly updates priorities based on context and experience. As engineers build better adaptive AI systems, these findings offer a reminder that intelligence in the brain is deeply dynamic, state-dependent and shaped by feedback.

FDA-cleared brain stimulation therapy posts striking PTSD remission results

A personalised magnetic brain stimulation system for post-traumatic stress disorder has reported a 68% remission rate in an early clinical trial, alongside a 52% reduction in symptoms. The treatment, known as Magnetic e-Resonance Therapy, or MeRT, has now received FDA clearance and may offer a new option for patients who have not responded well to medication or psychotherapy.

The Discovery/Development

The technology was developed by California-based Wave Neuroscience in collaboration with researchers at the Texas A&M Health Institute of Biosciences and Technology. Its central claim is straightforward but ambitious: rather than applying the same stimulation pattern to every patient, the system first measures an individual’s brain activity with an electroencephalogram, then uses those readings to guide targeted magnetic stimulation. Wave says this personalised approach produced the strong PTSD results reported in its clinical trial. According to the company, participants experienced a 52% reduction in symptoms and 68% achieved remission. The full findings have not yet been publicly released, but the therapy has now cleared an important regulatory hurdle.

Why This Matters

PTSD remains a major public health problem. The article notes that an estimated 6% of Americans will experience PTSD in their lifetime, with higher rates among women and veterans. In the United States, about 7% of veterans receive a PTSD diagnosis annually, and approximately 17.5 veterans die by suicide each day, underscoring the urgency of more effective care. Since PTSD was formally recognised as a diagnosis in 1980, treatment has largely relied on psychotherapy and medications such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, including sertraline and fluoxetine. These options can help, but the article says many patients continue to live with persistent symptoms despite treatment.

That gap helps explain why the new results are drawing attention. A 2020 review of PTSD medications found symptom improvements, but remission rates were no higher than 10.9%. Wave Neuroscience’s reported remission figure is therefore far above what is described in that medication literature, even if the company itself cautions that these are different studies and should not be treated as a head-to-head comparison. For patients and clinicians, the promise is not just improvement, but the possibility of reaching remission with a non-drug intervention.

How It Works

MeRT builds on transcranial magnetic stimulation, or TMS, a technique already used in psychiatry, including for treatment-resistant depression. In TMS, a coil placed against the scalp delivers magnetic pulses that influence brain activity. What distinguishes MeRT, according to the article, is that it does not use a standard protocol for all patients. Instead, it combines EEG data with an AI-assisted multivariate algorithm to tailor stimulation to each person’s brain activity profile.

The idea is rooted in precision medicine: small biological differences can matter a great deal. Dr. Kenneth S. Ramos of Texas A&M, who helped guide the research, argued that even though humans are roughly 99.9% genetically alike, the remaining differences can shape treatment response. He contrasted conventional TMS with MeRT using a roof analogy: the former is like covering a leak with a tarp, while the latter aims to identify and replace the damaged shingles causing the problem. In practical terms, MeRT is intended to be a more targeted intervention, informed by measurable brain signals rather than guesswork.

Implications and Future Directions

The clinical trial protocol itself suggests a relatively intensive course of care: participants received five sessions a week for five to six weeks. Researchers reported that the gains appeared to persist after the treatment period ended, though the article does not provide the detailed follow-up data. If those results hold up in fuller publication and broader study, the implications could be significant. The treatment could expand options for patients who struggle with medication side effects, who do not respond adequately to current drugs, or who find psychotherapy too difficult to complete because it can be anxiety-provoking and high-effort.

There is also a regulatory and practical story here. MeRT received FDA Breakthrough Device designation in 2024, a status intended to speed promising technologies through review without relaxing scientific standards. Dr. Rick Silva of Texas A&M said the project moved from start to clearance in just over two years, a pace he contrasted with the roughly 10 years that medical device development and evaluation can often take. The article also notes that Wave and Texas A&M are working with stakeholders to expand availability and make the therapy more affordable through insurance coverage. That may prove as important as the science itself: a treatment can only change care if patients can actually access it.

The company is also looking beyond PTSD. Wave’s chief medical officer, Erik Won, said the technology could eventually be adapted to depression and anxiety. If that happens, MeRT could become part of a wider move towards data-informed, individualised psychiatry, in which brain measurements help select the right intervention rather than forcing patients through trial-and-error treatment.

Expert Perspectives

Several of the study’s collaborators framed the clearance as a major shift in mental healthcare. Won described the findings as “a quantum change” and “an inflection point” for what can be offered to patients, while also pointing to the 68% remission rate as the most clinically meaningful figure. Dr. Israel Liberzon, the lead psychiatrist on the study, said the clearance would create the first non-medication option specifically for PTSD, noting that the FDA has approved only two medications for the disorder and that psychotherapy, while effective for some, can be difficult for many people to sustain. Dr. Leslie S. Prichep, Wave’s chief scientific officer, said the clearance reflects decades of work using electrophysiological biomarkers to identify abnormal brain activity and guide personalised intervention strategies.

At the same time, the article itself signals caution. It says the headline results are preliminary and the full findings are still being prepared for public release. It also explicitly notes that comparing the company’s trial results with the 2020 medication review would not be a direct comparison of treatment effectiveness. In other words, the data are encouraging, but they are not the same as a complete, peer-reviewed evidence base.

The Bigger Picture

The most interesting broader theme here is not simply that one PTSD treatment appears to work well, but that neuroscience is moving towards interventions that are both biologically measurable and individually tuned. That matters because psychiatry has long struggled with one-size-fits-all approaches in conditions that vary widely from patient to patient. MeRT’s pitch is that objective brain data can make mental health treatment more precise, less trial-and-error-driven, and less opaque to patients.

That ambition also intersects with a larger question about AI in medicine. The article describes an “augmented AI derived multivariate algorithm” used to personalise stimulation. In this case, AI is not replacing clinicians; it is helping translate complex biological signals into treatment settings. That is a useful model for the wider field: systems that support decision-making by extracting patterns from messy data, while remaining bounded by clinical oversight and regulatory review. Whether MeRT becomes a widely used PTSD therapy will depend on more than one promising trial, but the clearance suggests that precision psychiatry is no longer a speculative future. It is beginning to enter the clinic.
