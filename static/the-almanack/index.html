AI Safety Tightens as Agents Misfire, Coding Benchmarks Cool, and the Scaling Debate Returns

Today’s AI news paints a sharply dual picture: rapid technical progress on one side, and stubborn reliability problems on the other. New research suggests AI systems can spot misbehaviour in other agents with striking accuracy, yet reviewers are still overwhelmed by false alarms. At the same time, fresh benchmark results show that even the strongest coding agents continue to need substantial human clean-up, reinforcing the gap between headline performance and day-to-day usefulness.

Beyond safety and evaluation, the industry’s strategic arguments are becoming more visible. Richard Sutton’s long-running critique of raw scaling has resurfaced, as researchers and startups alike ask whether the next leap in AI will come from bigger models or genuinely new learning methods. Meanwhile, Anthropic’s IPO plans and a growing body of work on evals, corrigibility, and model interpretability suggest that the field is still racing ahead even as it wrestles with how to measure, govern, and trust its systems.

Key Developments

The dominant theme today is a widening divide between benchmark success and real-world robustness. Several stories point to a familiar pattern: models can perform impressively in controlled settings, but falter when exposed to messy, adversarial, or operationally relevant conditions. That includes coding agents that fail most real requests without human intervention, and safety tools that can detect bad behaviour yet still create too much noise to rely upon.

Another major thread is institutionalisation. There is more attention than ever on evaluations, corrigibility, and governance frameworks, alongside commercial signals such as Anthropic’s IPO push. In other words, AI is becoming less of a frontier experiment and more of a system that must be audited, funded, benchmarked, and regulated at scale.

Analysis

Today’s coverage reinforces a central truth of the current AI cycle: progress is real, but it is uneven and often poorly captured by headline benchmarks. The most advanced systems are becoming more capable, yet they still fail in ways that matter operationally, whether in coding, cyber, or oversight. That mismatch is now one of the main stories in AI governance, because it determines how quickly these systems can be safely deployed.

For readers watching the sector, the key trend is not just capability growth but institutional adaptation. Labs are building better evals, investors are financing even amid market pressure, and policy thinkers are grappling with problems that may not fit neatly into existing legal categories. The coming months will likely hinge on whether safety methods can move from promising research to reliable practice, and whether governments can regulate systems that are advancing faster than the rules meant to govern them.

AI coding agents still need human cleanup on nearly three out of four tasks in a real-world test

A new benchmark built from actual user change requests is puncturing a familiar AI story: coding agents that look near-perfect on older tests still struggle badly when asked to modify real software. In the strongest result reported, the best model fully completed only 26.4 per cent of tasks, meaning human developers still had to step in on the majority of jobs.

The Discovery/Development

Researchers from Alibaba and several Asian universities created Co-Design Bench, a new benchmark designed to measure how coding agents behave in realistic development work rather than in tidy laboratory conditions. The tasks come from actual user change requests made during coding sessions, including cases where people had to rephrase instructions several times or roll back changes because the software did not behave as intended.

According to the paper, the benchmark asks agents to rework existing web applications in response to requests that may include text, but also images, audio recordings, videos or documents. The team then grades the output strictly: one AI judge checks the finished app in a browser, and a second judge re-checks positive verdicts and looks for shortcuts such as mock-ups or hardcoded results. Only features that genuinely work earn credit.

The headline result is sobering. The best-performing model in the test, GPT-6 Astra, meets about 70 per cent of a task’s requirements on average, but fully completes only 26.4 per cent of tasks. In most cases, the agent does the bulk of the work but leaves at least one detail unfinished, which means a person still has to clean up the code by hand.

Why This Matters

The point of the benchmark is not simply to score AI systems lower. It is to show that older coding tests may no longer reflect the real difficulty of software work. The authors argue that many existing benchmarks have become too easy for agents that can run code, inspect the output and repair their own mistakes. On those tests, models can score above 99 per cent, which makes it difficult to separate one system from another and easy to overstate practical capability.

That gap matters for software teams and product leaders deciding whether to rely on autonomous coding tools. A model that excels on a benchmark can still be unreliable when the work involves multiple intertwined changes: layout, design, interaction and content all at once. In a real product environment, that kind of combined request is routine, not exceptional. Co-Design Bench is essentially an attempt to bring evaluation back into contact with how software is actually changed.

How It Works

Co-Design Bench is built around two tracks: a multimodal track and a repository track. The multimodal cases include tasks where the agent must extract values from receipts, tables or videos and then update every dependent view consistently. The repository track focuses on larger software overhauls inside an existing codebase.

The benchmark also highlights a point that is easy to miss in AI discussions: the environment matters almost as much as the model. In a separate experiment, the researchers ran the same models in different harnesses — the programs that let an agent read, write and execute code. Qwen3.8-27B scored 75 per cent in one environment and 56 per cent in another on larger overhauls, and the relative ranking of models changed depending on the harness.

That finding undercuts the usefulness of leaderboards that list only the model name. The paper’s recommendation is straightforward: report the model and the working environment together. For companies evaluating coding agents, that means testing the full setup as a pair, not just choosing the model with the highest published score.

Implications and Future Directions

The benchmark’s results suggest that current coding agents are still better understood as assistants than as independent software engineers. Even the best model, GPT-6 Astra, fully solved only 41 per cent of multimodal tasks and just under 18 per cent of repository tasks, according to the paper’s results reported by The Decoder. Other models — including Claude Opus 5 and Qwen3.8-Max — followed behind, while Grok 4.6 ranked last.

There is also an important efficiency story here. Extra compute did not reliably rescue weaker performance. GLM-5.2 used nearly six times as many tokens as GPT-6 Astra on smaller changes, yet still scored markedly worse. That suggests the bottleneck is not simply how much computation a model is allowed, but how well it can juggle the many dependent pieces of a real change request.

Those comparisons reinforce the central argument: benchmark scores can be misleading unless they resemble the conditions developers actually face. The new paper’s stricter grading and more realistic task selection are meant to narrow that gap, but the authors themselves acknowledge that no single test captures every aspect of real-world coding.

The Bigger Picture

The larger lesson extends beyond coding. In AI, as in many fields, systems often look strongest in controlled settings and weaker in messy ones. That is especially important in safety-critical or high-stakes contexts, where an apparently competent model can still fail in subtle, expensive or hard-to-detect ways.

For software development, the message is not that coding agents are useless. They clearly can complete substantial portions of work and may already be helpful for scaffolding, refactoring and routine edits. But the evidence in Co-Design Bench suggests that autonomy is still limited when tasks are real, multimodal and interdependent. For now, the human developer remains the final editor, debugger and accountability layer.

Inside the brain’s error monitor: how the insula could help brain-computer interfaces correct themselves

A new study in Nature Communications suggests that the human anterior insula does more than register mistakes: it carries motor and error-related signals that could help a brain-computer interface detect when it is drifting from a user’s intention and adjust course in real time. In epilepsy patients fitted with intracranial electrodes, researchers found high-frequency activity in this region emerged as the interface failed to match intended movement, pointing to a possible route towards more self-correcting prosthetic control.

The Discovery/Development

The study, led by Paul Weger, Sophia Gimple and colleagues from Maastricht University and partner institutions, used intracranial electroencephalography (iEEG) in patients with epilepsy to examine how the brain responds while operating a motor brain-computer interface, or BCI. The team did not just look for movement signals. They also sampled error-related activity across a distributed network, with the specific aim of understanding how the brain notices when an external device is not doing what the user intended. According to the abstract, the researchers observed “high-frequency population dynamics” in the anterior insula that then propagated towards the prefrontal cortex as the interface failed to follow the user’s intention.

That pattern mattered because it was not uniform across the brain. The authors report that frontoinsular recording sites preferentially encoded error-related activity, while centroparietal sites showed a mixture of compensatory motor signals and error signals. In the study’s framing, that makes the frontoinsular region a particularly compelling target for real-time error correction in BCIs. The paper’s central claim is that the system can harness insular brain activity to integrate cognitive processes directly into device control, rather than relying on motor decoding alone.

Why This Matters

BCIs often succeed or fail on a simple problem: they must infer a user’s intention from noisy neural activity, and then translate that intention into an external action. But intention is not the whole story. When the machine misses, the brain has to notice the mismatch and adapt. That is why error recognition is so important, both biologically and technologically. The abstract describes it as “fundamental to adaptive behaviour,” enabling rapid compensatory action when outcomes deviate from expectations.

This study matters because it links three things that are usually analysed separately: neural coding, error monitoring and closed-loop prosthetic control. If a BCI can detect not only what a person wants to do, but also when the device has gone wrong, it may become more reliable and more intuitive. In practical terms, that could reduce the burden on users who currently have to compensate manually for decoding errors. In scientific terms, it strengthens the idea that usable prosthetic systems may need to borrow from the brain’s own performance-monitoring circuitry, not just its motor commands.

How It Works

The key technical idea is that the insula may serve as a heterogeneous hub linking action and outcome. The authors say they found spatially organised insular responses to both error processing and movement feedback. That distinction is important: a brain region can be active during movement without specifically signalling that something has gone wrong. The study suggests the anterior insula is doing both, but in a structured way that separates error-related activity from other motor signals.

To identify these signals, the researchers recorded iEEG directly from the brains of epilepsy patients. Intracranial recording offers a much finer view than scalp EEG because electrodes sit closer to the neural tissue, allowing the team to track fast, high-frequency dynamics. In this case, those dynamics appeared in the anterior insula when the interface diverged from intention, and then spread towards prefrontal areas associated with higher-order control. The study also found that centroparietal regions were less cleanly organised: they contained both compensatory motor signals and error-related activity, which makes them less straightforward as a target for decoding error alone.

The implication is subtle but important. A future BCI may not need to guess error from motor output alone. Instead, it could listen for a dedicated neural warning signal from frontoinsular regions and use that information to correct the device’s behaviour while the task is still underway. That is the “self-correcting” idea in the paper’s title: a closed-loop interface that does not merely execute commands, but monitors and adjusts its own performance based on the user’s brain signals.

There are also broader neuroscientific implications. The insula has long been recognised as a multifunctional region involved in integrating internal bodily states, cognition and action. This paper adds to that picture by identifying spatially organised insular responses that appear to distinguish movement from error processing. That makes the region look less like a vague “integration centre” and more like a structured node in a network for performance monitoring.

At the same time, the work is early and grounded in a highly specific setting: epilepsy patients already undergoing intracranial monitoring. That is an important strength for signal quality, but it also means the findings come from a clinical population and a research context that may not generalise straightforwardly to all users of BCIs. The abstract does not provide evidence here of a deployed, long-term prosthetic system in everyday use; rather, it demonstrates the neural basis that could support one. The leap from recorded error signals to robust, clinically useful self-correcting devices remains a translation challenge.

Expert Perspectives

The article itself does not include quoted outside commentary, but the authors’ interpretation is clear in the abstract: the frontoinsular sites were “a compelling target for real-time error correction,” and the overall findings “demonstrate a human intracranial BCI harnessing insular brain activity.” The acknowledgements also emphasise the practical realities of such work, thanking patients and technicians at the Kempenhaeghe epilepsy monitoring unit for contributions made beyond the regular clinical schedule.

The Bigger Picture

This study sits at the intersection of neuroscience and a familiar AI safety question: how does a system know when it is wrong? In machine learning, error detection and correction are central to robustness. In the brain, the same problem has evolved into performance monitoring, feedback processing and adaptive control. What makes this paper notable is that it treats those biological mechanisms not just as objects of study, but as resources for interface design.

That has a wider significance for neurotechnology. The more BCIs move from lab prototypes to clinical tools, the more they will need to handle uncertainty, drift and mismatch between user intention and device action. A self-correcting interface built around insular error coding would not solve every problem, but it suggests a more intelligent design philosophy: one that lets the machine participate in the brain’s own monitoring loop. In that sense, the study is not only about decoding neurons. It is about learning how the nervous system notices failure — and how a prosthetic system might learn to do the same.
