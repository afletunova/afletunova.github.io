What Should a Student Model Learn First? Theory Reframes a Long-Running Question in Distillation

A new theoretical analysis of on-policy distillation tackles a deceptively simple question at the heart of modern model refinement: when a student model learns from a stronger teacher, which tokens should matter, in what order should they be learned, and how quickly should that learning happen? The paper argues that these choices are not just engineering details — they shape the objective itself.

The Discovery/Development

The paper, Which Tokens, in What Order, and How Fast? A Theoretical Analysis of On-Policy Distillation Objectives, examines the mechanics of distillation in the setting where a student model is trained on the outputs it actually produces, rather than on a fixed dataset of teacher demonstrations. That distinction matters because the student’s own predictions influence the training trajectory, making the learning process more dynamic and more sensitive to design choices than ordinary supervised fine-tuning.

At its core, the work asks what an on-policy distillation objective is really optimising. The title signals the paper’s central contribution: it breaks the problem into three connected questions — which tokens should be prioritised, in what sequence they should be incorporated, and how rapidly the student should shift towards the teacher’s distribution. By treating those choices theoretically rather than as ad hoc implementation details, the paper aims to explain why some distillation setups work better than others and what they are implicitly encouraging a model to learn.

Why This Matters

Distillation has become one of the quiet workhorses of modern AI training. It is used to compress large models into smaller ones, improve efficiency, and refine behaviour after pretraining. In practice, though, many post-training pipelines rely on heuristics: they choose what data to distil on, how strongly to weight certain outputs, and how aggressively to update the student, often with limited theoretical justification. This paper addresses that gap by analysing the objective itself.

That matters for more than academic neatness. In post-training, the details of how a model learns can affect not only accuracy, but also robustness, calibration, and the preservation — or loss — of desirable behaviour from the teacher. If on-policy distillation is a major route for model refinement, then understanding its mechanics helps explain why systems improve in some ways while drifting in others. The paper’s framing therefore speaks to a broader challenge in AI: as models become more capable, the key questions increasingly concern how they are trained after pretraining, not just how large they are.

How It Works

“On-policy” means the student learns from its own generated outputs rather than only from pre-collected examples. In practical terms, the student makes a prediction, the teacher provides a target signal for that prediction, and training updates the student based on the tokens or actions it actually chose. This creates a feedback loop: the student’s present behaviour shapes the data it sees next.

The paper’s theoretical analysis focuses on token-level decisions within that loop. If a model produces a sequence, the question is not merely whether the final answer matches the teacher, but which intermediate tokens are most important to correct first. The ordering matters because earlier tokens can influence later ones, especially in autoregressive models where each new token depends on what came before. The speed matters because a gradual shift towards the teacher may preserve useful structure, while a faster shift could force alignment more abruptly.

By examining these dimensions together, the authors illuminate a subtle point: distillation objectives do not simply transfer knowledge, they encode a training policy over sequence generation. In other words, the learner is not just being told what to say, but implicitly which parts of what it says deserve the most attention, and at what pace the teacher’s preferences should overwrite the student’s current tendencies.

Implications and Future Directions

The immediate implication is that distillation should be understood as an objective with structure, not a single monolithic recipe. If the order of token learning and the rate of updating are both theoretically meaningful, then practitioners may be able to design more deliberate post-training pipelines instead of tuning them by trial and error. That could matter for efficiency, sample quality, and the stability of the student model’s behaviour.

There is also a broader methodological lesson. As AI systems are increasingly refined through layered training stages — pretraining, supervised instruction tuning, preference optimisation, distillation, and other forms of alignment — each stage may introduce its own hidden assumptions. A theory of on-policy distillation helps expose those assumptions. It suggests that some performance differences between pipelines may arise not from mysterious “model magic”, but from the ordering and weighting of the learning process itself.

The paper also points to open questions. If token order matters, then different tasks may demand different curricula inside the distillation objective. If learning speed matters, then there may be no single best schedule for all models or domains. Those are not minor implementation choices; they are design variables that could shape how future systems inherit — or fail to inherit — the strengths of their teachers.

The Bigger Picture

Viewed more broadly, this research fits a growing trend in AI towards understanding post-training as a principled scientific problem rather than a bag of heuristics. That shift is especially important in AI safety work, where the concern is not simply whether a model performs well on benchmarks, but whether training procedures reliably produce systems that retain intended behaviours under distribution shift, feedback loops, and complex deployment conditions.

On-policy distillation sits at the intersection of efficiency and alignment. It is a mechanism for transferring capability, but also a mechanism for shaping behaviour. By asking “which tokens, in what order, and how fast?”, the paper highlights a deeper issue in contemporary AI: the smallest details of optimisation can carry large consequences for what a model becomes. In that sense, the work is less about a narrow technical tweak than about the architecture of learning itself — how a model is nudged, sequence by sequence, towards one policy rather than another.

A Missing “Brake” Gene Sends Developing Brain Cells Into Overdrive

A single gene, Pten, acts like a molecular brake on the developing brain. New work from researchers at the Institute of Science and Technology Austria (ISTA) shows that when individual brain stem cells lose that brake, they produce too many neurons and astrocytes, and the cells they make grow unusually large — a cellular route to brain overgrowth.

The Discovery/Development

The study, published in Science Advances, was led by Simon Hippenmeyer’s group at ISTA in collaboration with researchers at Sorbonne Université, Yale School of Medicine and Vetmeduni Vienna. To see what happens when Pten is lost in only a few developing brain cells, the team used a lineage-tracing method called Mosaic Analysis with Double Markers, or MADM. This technique allowed them to delete Pten in a sparse set of mouse cortical radial glial stem cells while fluorescently tagging those cells and all of their descendants.

That design mattered. When a gene is removed throughout the whole brain in animal models, the effects can be so severe that the organism does not survive long enough to study development in detail. By tracking individual “family trees” of cells instead, the researchers were able to map the chain reaction set off by Pten loss at single-cell resolution. The result: a clearer picture of how one mutation can alter both the number and type of cells produced during cortical development.

Why This Matters

PTEN mutations in humans are associated with a cluster of conditions known as PTEN Hamartoma Tumor Syndrome, which includes megalencephaly — an unusually enlarged brain — as well as autism spectrum disorder, intellectual disability, epilepsy and brain tumours. Yet the precise developmental logic behind these outcomes has been hard to pin down. This study offers a concrete cellular explanation for one major feature of the syndrome: the brain can grow too large because individual stem cells generate too many descendant cells, and those cells themselves are enlarged.

The findings also matter beyond developmental neuroscience. PTEN-mutant patients face an increased risk of gliomas, aggressive brain tumours with a glial origin. By showing how Pten loss changes glial production through a specific signalling detour, the work points to a possible vulnerability in tumours that stop responding to standard therapies.

How It Works

During normal brain development, radial glial progenitor cells divide and then gradually shift into different fates, producing neurons first and glial cells such as astrocytes later. PTEN helps regulate that process by restraining cell growth and division. In the new study, when Pten was removed from individual radial glial cells, those cells produced about 25% more neurons than normal. They also generated substantially more astrocytes, and both cell types were abnormally enlarged.

The astrocyte result is especially important because it revealed a signalling reroute. Under ordinary developmental conditions, radial glial cells rely on the epidermal growth factor receptor, or EGFR, pathway to become astrocytes. But when the researchers switched off both Pten and Egfr in the same cells, astrocyte overproduction continued. That told them the system was bypassing the usual route. Molecular analysis showed that Pten-deficient progenitors had shifted to the MAPK pathway instead — a different intracellular signalling cascade that can act as an alternative route for glial differentiation.

In practical terms, the study suggests that Pten does not merely “slow down” development in a vague sense. It helps keep distinct stages of cortical cell production properly sequenced. When that control is lost, stem cells do not just make too many cells; they may also use a different signalling logic to decide what kind of cells to make.

Implications and Future Directions

The immediate implication is conceptual: brain overgrowth is not necessarily the result of a single, blunt increase in proliferation. It can emerge from a precise combination of effects — more progeny, altered lineage progression and larger cell size. That kind of mechanistic clarity is valuable because it gives researchers a more testable framework for understanding why PTEN mutations are linked to megalencephaly and related neurodevelopmental disorders.

Expert Perspectives

First author Osvaldo Miranda Romero, a postdoctoral researcher in Hippenmeyer’s laboratory at ISTA, said the field had lacked “basic information about what Pten mutations mean at the level of individual cells and how they affect cellular output.” The lineage-tracing approach, he explained, was designed to answer precisely that question: how many cells a stem cell makes, and what kinds of cells they are.

Romero also underscored the significance of the signalling reroute, noting that EGFR is an important driver of glial-cell formation under normal conditions, while MAPK can serve as “a kind of alternative route under exceptional circumstances.” That alternative route may be biologically useful in development, but in the wrong context it could also help tumours escape therapies aimed at EGFR.

The Bigger Picture

One of the most important lessons from this work is that complex brain disorders can begin with very local changes in cell behaviour. A mutation in a single gene, inside a small number of stem cells, can shift the balance of an entire tissue. That is a reminder of how much of neuroscience depends on developmental timing: when cells divide, when they differentiate, and when they stop.

The study also reflects a broader trend in neuroscience towards single-cell and lineage-based methods that can untangle cause and effect rather than just catalog correlations. For the field, that means moving from broad labels such as “overgrowth” or “dysregulated development” to identifiable cellular routes and molecular checkpoints. For patients and clinicians, the hope is more modest but more concrete: that understanding those routes may eventually yield better ways to diagnose PTEN-related disorders, anticipate tumour behaviour and design therapies that are less easy for cancer cells to bypass.
