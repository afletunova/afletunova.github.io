AI Claims a Breakthrough on Riemann’s 165-Year-Old Puzzle — and Stanford Answers Within Hours

In a dramatic day for mathematics, an Anthropic researcher said Claude had helped produce a proof of the Generalised Riemann Hypothesis, one of the deepest unsolved problems in number theory. Within hours, Stanford mathematicians published a separate, all-human draft on the same target, turning a potential AI milestone into a live test of how frontier models and human mathematicians compare under the harshest scrutiny.

The Discovery/Development

Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, announced on Friday that he believed he had “working with Claude” discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis — more precisely, the Generalised Riemann Hypothesis (GRH), a broader form of the famous conjecture. He posted a paper and accompanying computer code on his website, and said the project had begun as a long-term personal goal before accelerating over the past few months with the help of the AI model Claude. According to Alpöge, the model did much of the heavy lifting near the end, after he had guided the direction and tested a simplified toy version of the problem.

Hours later, Stanford professor and American Mathematical Society president Ravi Vakil posted what he called a “hurried announcement” of an independent draft proof by mathematician Alessandro Masullo. Vakil emphasised that Masullo’s draft was “all human, with no AI,” and said the work had already been under extended review by Stanford number theorist Brian Conrad, who had been providing feedback for months. The timing changed because Anthropic’s announcement forced Stanford’s hand; Masullo had originally planned to spend another year refining the draft before making it public.

Why This Matters

The Riemann Hypothesis is not just a hard question in mathematics; it is one of the field’s central organising ideas. Proposed by Bernhard Riemann in 1859, it sits at the heart of understanding the distribution of prime numbers, the indivisible building blocks of arithmetic. A proof would confirm hundreds of results that mathematicians have already built on the assumption that the hypothesis is true, and because the GRH is a broader version of the same idea, the impact would extend into wider areas of number theory and geometry.

The stakes are also cultural and institutional. In 2000, the Clay Mathematics Institute made the Riemann Hypothesis one of its seven Millennium Problems, attaching a $1 million prize to a solution. In a field where major breakthroughs can take decades, the possibility that an AI system has helped reach the finish line raises a larger question: are advanced models becoming genuine research partners, or merely highly capable accelerators for work humans still need to verify?

How It Works

The source article describes Alpöge and Claude’s approach as an extension of a method originally used by mathematician Stepanov in a different mathematical setting. The key challenge is that the usual tools of calculus — especially derivatives — work naturally on smooth continuous curves, but not on sequences of whole numbers. Alpöge and Claude reportedly sidestepped that obstacle by chaining together special mathematical transformations in a way that mimics differentiation. Claude’s crucial insight, Alpöge said, was that for a sequence of length X, one needs exactly as many chained transformations as the square root of X.

That sounds abstract, but the underlying issue is familiar to mathematicians: finding a bridge between discrete objects like primes and analytic tools that can reveal hidden structure. The Riemann Hypothesis itself is tied to the Riemann zeta function, which mathematicians use to study the “zeros” of a kind of calculating machine on a two-dimensional plane. Riemann’s conjecture was that all the important non-trivial zeros lie on a single vertical line at the horizontal value one-half. If true, that regularity would explain why primes look random yet obey deep statistical patterns.

Alpöge said the proof is too large to read in ordinary prose — printed out, it would run to more than 250,000 pages — so he released it in the Lean programming language, split into 14 data packages. Lean acts as a formal checker: it verifies, step by step, whether each logical move follows from the axioms. That offers one of the strongest possible safeguards against error, though it still depends on whether the original theorem was translated correctly into code.

But the source material also makes clear how provisional such claims remain. Vakil noted, just hours after his post, that several people had already reported problems in the proposed proof, some identified with the help of AI models. As he wrote, the community must resolve all issues before the proof can be accepted. In other words, the race is not over when an AI says it has solved a problem; in mathematics, the final arbiter is still communal verification.

Expert Perspectives

The reactions quoted in the source reveal both excitement and caution. Rutgers mathematician Alex Kontorovich compared the prime numbers to an orchestra, saying the Riemann Hypothesis asserts that “all these different instruments in the orchestra of the primes play at exactly the same volume.” Stanford’s Brian Conrad offered a different analogy, describing primes as serving a role in number theory akin to forces in physics. The article also cites MIT’s Andrew Sutherland, who said at a Harvard workshop in 2024: “An AI that can prove the Riemann hypothesis is not one I’d want to meet. If that happens, mathematicians having jobs will be the least of our problems.”

Alpöge himself struck an ambivalent note. He admitted that Claude had moved so far ahead of him in some subfields that he is presenting a result whose surrounding mathematics he has not fully mastered. Yet he also framed the work as part of a larger shift. “What I saw during the course of progress so amazed me that I believe it is one dawn across science of something far greater,” he said.

The Bigger Picture

This episode lands in the middle of a fast-moving year for AI in mathematics. The article notes that Claude had already been credited with several open-maths breakthroughs in 2026, including a Claude-generated solution to the Jacobian conjecture, a dispute over the Navier-Stokes equations, and a major OpenAI release claiming results on more than 100 long-standing math problems. Alpöge even said OpenAI’s earlier work helped inspire parts of his own proof.

That broader trend matters for AI safety as well as research. Mathematics is one of the clearest domains for judging whether a model can reason reliably, because every step can, in principle, be checked. If AI systems are beginning to generate serious candidate proofs in one of the most demanding areas of pure thought, then the field is moving from debating whether models can assist science to deciding how to verify and govern their contributions. The Riemann episode does not settle that question. It sharpens it.

How a Liver Tumour Rewires a Key Immune Receptor: The Shape-Shifting gp130 Mutations Driving Disease

A new structural study has revealed how cancer-linked mutations in gp130, a cell-surface signalling receptor, can switch it on without its usual ligand. Using cryo-electron microscopy and mutagenesis, researchers showed that most inflammatory hepatocellular adenoma-associated mutants assemble into unusual “X”-shaped dimers, while one rarer mutant adopts a distinct “Y”-shaped form that activates signalling by a different route. The work offers a molecular explanation for why these liver tumours become addicted to aberrant gp130 activity and points to new strategies for blocking it.

The Discovery/Development

Inflammatory hepatocellular adenomas (IHCAs) are benign liver tumours that are “primarily driven” by somatic mutations in gp130, according to the paper published in Nature Communications. The receptor, also known as IL6ST, is a component of cytokine signalling pathways and normally requires external ligand engagement to trigger downstream responses. The challenge for the authors was to understand how tumour-associated mutants override that control.

To do so, the team studied five gp130 mutants found in human IHCAs and two engineered variants that also show ligand-independent activity. They combined cryo-electron microscopy with mutagenesis to visualise the receptor assemblies and test which contacts were necessary for activation. The result is a detailed structural map of constitutive gp130 signalling: most of the mutants did not simply mimic the normal receptor, but instead formed unexpected dimeric architectures that appear to lock the receptor into an active state.

Why This Matters

Although IHCAs are described as benign, the biology behind them is anything but trivial. Constitutive receptor activation means that cells receive persistent growth- and inflammation-related signals without the external cues that ordinarily keep the system in check. That makes gp130 a useful case study in how a single membrane protein, when subtly altered, can drive disease through misregulated communication rather than wholesale destruction of tissue architecture.

The work matters beyond one tumour type because gp130 sits at the crossroads of interleukin signalling. The authors note that their findings provide “a foundation for developing therapeutic strategies targeting aberrant gp130 signalling in IHCAs”. In other words, the study does more than identify mutations: it offers a mechanistic basis for drug design. If researchers can identify the structural features that keep mutant gp130 switched on, they may be able to interrupt them selectively, rather than broadly suppressing the cytokine pathways that healthy tissues also need.

How It Works

The central insight is that the oncogenic mutants do not all activate gp130 in the same way. Most of the variants studied formed “X”-shaped dimers through what the authors describe as a 3D domain-swapping mechanism. In simple terms, parts of two receptor molecules interlock in a way that brings them into a stable signalling-competent arrangement. These dimers feature large separations between the juxtamembrane domains — the regions just outside the cell membrane that often help transmit conformational changes inward.

Instead of relying on a tight, direct juxtamembrane contact, these “X”-shaped dimers depend on D1 domain-mediated “tip-to-tip” interactions. The D1 domain is part of the receptor’s extracellular region, and in these mutants it appears to help dimer clusters form and stabilise the active state. That clustering is important: it suggests the receptor’s activation is not merely about one bond or one interface, but about a higher-order assembly that holds multiple pieces in the right orientation for signalling.

The exception is especially revealing. A rare mutant adopted a different “Y”-shaped conformation, with closely aligned juxtamembrane domains. Unlike the majority class, this structure allows direct activation without the need for dimer clustering. The existence of this second mechanism shows that “oncogenic gp130” is not a single structural category; rather, different mutations can hijack the same receptor through distinct physical arrangements. That diversity may help explain why the disease biology is so robust.

Implications and Future Directions

The study’s main implication is practical: structural biology can turn a descriptive mutation catalogue into a framework for intervention. By showing which receptor interfaces are involved in constitutive activation, the authors have created potential targets for therapeutics that could disrupt the abnormal assemblies rather than the receptor globally. That distinction matters in cytokine biology, where blanket inhibition can create unwanted immune effects.

At the same time, the paper is careful in scope. The authors studied five representative IHCA-associated mutants and two engineered ligand-independent mutants. That gives a strong mechanistic window into the problem, but it is still a sample rather than a complete survey of all possible gp130 alterations. The rare “Y”-shaped form is particularly important because it reminds readers that structural rules inferred from one set of mutants may not generalise cleanly to every oncogenic variant.

Expert Perspectives

The article itself does not quote external commentators, but it does make the authors’ position clear. Their conclusion is that the mutants show “diversity of gp130 mutant activation mechanisms” and that this diversity should inform future therapeutic strategy. The acknowledgement that the project was funded by Regeneron Pharmaceuticals is relevant context for readers assessing the translational orientation of the work; the authors also disclose that they own options and/or stock of the company, and that two senior authors are officers of Regeneron.

The Bigger Picture

For neuroscience and biomedicine more broadly, the paper is a reminder that signalling pathology often begins with structure. Cells make decisions by converting geometry into chemistry: receptor shape, spacing, and clustering determine whether a pathway stays quiet or turns on. That principle is central not only to cancer biology but also to neuroscience, where membrane receptors, interleukin pathways, and inflammatory signalling can influence brain function, immune-brain interactions, and disease states.

There is also a conceptual parallel with AI safety: both fields increasingly depend on understanding hidden mechanisms rather than observing outputs alone. Just as a model’s behaviour can look stable while its internal representations produce unexpected failures, a receptor can appear ordinary at the surface while its molecular architecture has shifted into a self-activating state. In both cases, the lesson is the same: durable control requires structural understanding, not just phenomenological description.
