The real significance of AI’s maths breakthroughs may be what they reveal about machine capability

A former OpenAI researcher says the headline-grabbing question is not whether an AI can prove something as famous as the Riemann hypothesis, but the fact that it can produce advanced mathematical work at all. In Scott Aaronson’s view, these results hint at a much larger shift: AI systems may be crossing into territory that could reshape science, cryptography, the economy, and even global security.

The Discovery/Development

In a recent blog post, Scott Aaronson, a complexity theorist and professor at the University of Texas at Austin who previously worked at OpenAI, reflected on a series of mathematical advances attributed to AI systems. The immediate example he discusses is an OpenAI proof about matrix multiplication. Rather than treating it as a single isolated achievement, Aaronson uses it to argue that AI is beginning to uncover a broader landscape of mathematical truth that humans had only dimly perceived before.

The matrix multiplication result matters because it includes a matching lower bound to an existing fast algorithm. In plain terms, OpenAI did not merely find a new way to compute something more quickly; it also showed how far that process can be accelerated before hitting a fundamental limit, at least in the mathematical model it studied. Aaronson notes that OpenAI produced a similar result for the Fourier transform, a core tool used in audio, image and signal processing. Again, the significance lies not just in speed, but in demonstrating the boundary of what is possible.

These lower bounds, Aaronson stresses, are especially important. A new algorithm can set a record and then be surpassed. A lower bound, by contrast, tells researchers where further gains run into hard constraints. That is why, in his telling, the development is not really about one proof or one theorem, but about AI systems becoming capable of contributing to a class of problems that mathematicians have historically regarded as exceptionally difficult.

Why This Matters

Aaronson’s central argument is that the practical value of any single proof may be limited, but the broader signal is profound. Proving the Riemann hypothesis, for example, would not obviously transform everyday life, he writes. The downstream applications of such work, if any, could emerge much later and in ways that are impossible to predict in advance. Yet the fact that an AI system can produce advanced work in this vicinity suggests a more important change: machine intelligence is no longer confined to narrow, repetitive tasks or pattern recognition alone.

That is why Aaronson says the implications could range from the mundane to the catastrophic. At one end, these systems might help reshape scientific research, engineering and the global economy by accelerating the discovery of useful methods and limits. At the other, they could present existential risks. He does not present those possibilities as settled conclusions, but as serious possibilities that anyone engaged with frontier AI should consider rather than dismiss.

His criticism of what he calls the “‘ooga booga, me only care about Riemann proof if it help me make sharper spear’ caveman act” is aimed at the idea that mathematical breakthroughs only matter if they immediately translate into practical tools. For Aaronson, that misses the point. The more important question is what such results say about the general capabilities of AI systems and how quickly those capabilities may be expanding.

How It Works

The technical thread running through Aaronson’s analysis is complexity theory, the study of how much time and computational power problems require as they scale. One of the field’s best-known open questions is P versus NP, which asks whether every problem whose answer can be checked quickly by a computer can also be solved quickly. Most researchers suspect the answer is no, but no proof exists. Aaronson says the new AI-driven results have changed how he thinks about that problem.

He writes that he now feels, “for the first time ever,” that a proof of P ≠ NP is not far off. That is a notable statement from a researcher in the field, though it remains his judgement rather than an established conclusion. The reason the AI work matters is that it appears to be uncovering both constructive results — new algorithms — and barrier results — lower bounds that show where those algorithms cannot go much further. Together, those two kinds of results help map the terrain of what computation can and cannot do.

He also says that, in October, he wrote that AI labs were testing whether their models could break cryptographic systems. Taken together, these remarks suggest that the frontier is not just about generating elegant proofs; it may also involve stress-testing the foundations of modern security. That possibility is part of why the article frames the issue as more than an academic milestone.

At the same time, the story leaves important uncertainties unresolved. Aaronson’s comments are reflective and forward-looking, not proof that cryptographic systems have already been broken or that P ≠ NP is near. The specific power of the AI systems involved, the exact methods they used, and the generality of their success are not laid out in the piece. What is clear is that he sees the trajectory itself — towards increasingly sophisticated mathematical output — as the more consequential development.

Expert Perspectives

The article’s main expert voice is Aaronson himself, whose experience gives his comments weight. He is not only a former OpenAI researcher but also a prominent complexity theorist, which makes his reaction especially noteworthy. His language is unusually direct: he argues that people should abandon the instinct to dismiss these results unless they deliver immediate practical gains.

That said, the piece also signals that opinions differ. Aaronson explicitly acknowledges disagreement over how to interpret the significance of a proof like the Riemann hypothesis. Some observers may focus narrowly on whether a result has immediate use; others, like Aaronson, see it as evidence of a deeper shift in machine capability. The article does not quote additional dissenting experts, but it does make clear that the debate is not merely technical — it is about what society should take from AI systems that can now participate in advanced theoretical work.

The Bigger Picture

Seen in the broader context of AI safety, this story captures a familiar but increasingly urgent theme: capability jumps often matter less for the individual benchmark than for what they reveal about general-purpose power. A system that can help solve hard mathematical problems may also be closer to mastering other domains where reasoning, abstraction and strategic planning are crucial. That is why the same development can excite mathematicians, unsettle cryptographers and alarm AI safety researchers at once.

The article also reflects a shift in how frontier AI is discussed. Earlier debates often centred on whether models could write convincing text or complete common coding tasks. Aaronson’s comments point to a more consequential threshold: AI systems producing work that touches the deepest open problems in mathematics. Whether or not any single result changes daily life, the overall trajectory may matter enormously. In that sense, the Riemann hypothesis is not the story; the story is the kind of machine that might eventually prove it.

Scientists rethink a basic rule of brain science: most neurons may be generalists, not specialists

For decades, neuroscience has often treated brain cells as if each had a single job: a neuron for colour, another for shape, another for movement, and so on. A new study in Nature challenges that organising principle, finding that most neurons across the mouse cortex respond to multiple kinds of information in distinct ways — more Swiss Army knife than single-purpose tool.

The Discovery/Development

Researchers at Columbia University’s Zuckerman Institute, working with data from the International Brain Laboratory consortium, analysed recordings from individual neurons across 43 regions of the mouse cerebral cortex. All of the mice were studied performing the same type of task, giving the team a more consistent basis for comparison than many earlier experiments, which often differed in species, brain region or behavioural task.

The result, published in Nature as “Rarely categorical, highly separable representations along the cortical hierarchy”, was a striking reversal of the usual picture. Some neurons were clearly specialised, especially in primary sensory areas such as vision. But across much of the cortex, the researchers found that most neurons were not narrowly dedicated to one variable. Instead, they responded to a broad range of information, often in ways that looked unique rather than interchangeable.

“We have to move away from this image of the brain as a machine made of gears, with every gear having an exact purpose that we can attach a label to,” said Stefano Fusi, a principal investigator at Columbia’s Zuckerman Institute and co-senior author of the study. “The brain doesn’t work like that. Instead, most neurons can display a huge diversity of responses, and this can help the brain solve a huge number of different tasks.”

Why This Matters

The question at the heart of the study is one of neuroscience’s oldest: are neurons specialists or generalists? The answer matters because it shapes how scientists think about brain organisation, information processing and the meaning of neural recordings. If most neurons are tightly linked to one function, then identifying what a cell “means” may be relatively straightforward. If instead a single cell contributes to several kinds of computation at once, then the brain’s code is far harder to decode.

That question has practical consequences. Neuroscience has long relied on isolating single neurons and trying to infer what each one represents. But if a neuron can simultaneously carry information about, say, an object’s colour and its shape, then looking at it in isolation may be misleading. The study suggests that a fuller understanding comes from analysing populations of neurons together — much as the brain itself appears to process information through collective patterns rather than solitary cells.

It also helps explain how the brain can be both organised and flexible. The cortex is arranged into distinct regions with different roles, yet the new findings suggest that the flexibility of individual neurons may be a major ingredient in that system’s computational power.

How It Works

The researchers argue that many neurons act as part of “high-dimensional” representations. In plain terms, that means groups of neurons can encode several features at once, rather than assigning one cell to one feature. A neuron might respond to both a stimulus’s colour and its shape; a population of such neurons can then represent a red circle differently from a black square, even if the same cells are participating in both distinctions.

This kind of coding gives the brain room to reuse the same neural machinery across different tasks. As Fusi put it, “You can reuse these high-dimensional representations for lots of different tasks.” That reuse may be one reason the brain can adapt to so many situations without having to build a separate circuit for every new challenge.

The study also found that versatility does not mean uniformity. Even when neurons belonged to the same functional module, their activity patterns were rarely identical. Shuqi Wang, a co-lead author and doctoral student at École Polytechnique Fédérale de Lausanne, said each neuron is “versatile in its own way”. That diversity, the authors suggest, may be part of what makes neural computation so rich.

Implications and Future Directions

The most immediate implication is methodological. Neuroscientists may need to move further away from the habit of interpreting neurons one at a time and instead focus on patterns across populations. The paper explicitly frames this as a shift away from a decades-long tendency to discard neurons whose responses were too complex to fit a neat label.

There are also implications for how researchers interpret brain disorders and recovery. If normal brain function depends on flexible, overlapping representations rather than rigid one-to-one mappings, then disruptions may be more subtle than a simple loss of a single specialised cell type. In principle, that could affect how scientists think about repairing damaged circuits or restoring function after injury or disease.

The authors are now collaborating with Ueli Rutishauser’s group at Caltech to ask whether human neurons show similar versatility. They also want to know whether the balance between specialisation and generality changes depending on the demands of a task. For now, the evidence comes from mice, and the study’s conclusions are strongest for the cortical recordings and conditions examined here. But the central claim is substantial enough to reopen a long-running debate about how neural coding works.

Expert Perspectives

The paper’s authors are unusually direct about the conceptual break with past thinking. Posani, the study’s co-lead author and a principal investigator at the Paris Brain Institute and France’s CNRS, said the field has approached the problem under “widely different experimental conditions”, which helps explain why previous work produced conflicting results. This study’s strength, he argued, is that it used the same type of task across a very large set of brain regions.

Fusi was equally emphatic that specialisation still exists, but should be seen differently. “We’re not saying that there are no specialized neurons,” he said. “We’re saying they are the exceptions. They’re not the rule.”

The Bigger Picture

The findings resonate beyond neuroscience. Modern AI systems, especially large neural networks, also rely heavily on distributed representations rather than single units with fixed meanings. In that sense, the study strengthens a broader picture of intelligence — biological and artificial — as emerging from overlapping, reusable patterns rather than tidy symbolic parts. That does not mean the brain works like an AI model, but it does underline a shared lesson: complex cognition may depend on dense, flexible encodings that are hard to read out from one unit at a time.

It is also a reminder that some of neuroscience’s most basic assumptions remain provisional. Even after decades of study, the brain can still overturn simple metaphors. The cortex may indeed contain specialists, but this new work suggests that its dominant architecture is far more versatile than the old “one cell, one function” story implied.
