OpenAI’s AI has produced purported proofs in maths, computer science and physics — now outsiders must decide whether they hold

OpenAI says its internal models have generated results that, if correct, would unsettle long-standing assumptions in computer science, extend a major theorem in wave theory and strengthen the mathematical foundations of quantum field theory. The company has now enlisted outside experts — including a Nobel laureate and, in one case, the very researchers who proposed the original conjecture — to verify whether the AI’s proofs are sound.

The Discovery/Development

In a blog post, OpenAI said it has produced three research manuscripts from its internal models, spanning mathematics, theoretical computer science and physics, and has asked external specialists to act as “shepherds” for each result. The company describes the manuscripts as a kind of raw research data: promising enough to release, but not yet ready to be treated as accepted science. To support verification, OpenAI says it has translated the mathematics and computer science proofs into Lean, a programming language used to check proofs step by step. That does not settle the matter on its own, however. Lean can confirm that a proof follows the formal rules written into it; it cannot tell researchers whether the formal statement truly captures the intended problem.

The most striking claim concerns computer science. OpenAI says its system has proved that the Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH) is false — a result that, if validated, would overturn a widely used assumption about the hardness of the Boolean satisfiability problem, or SAT. In mathematics, the company says its models have produced a proof of the local smoothing conjecture in every dimension, extending a result previously proved only in two dimensions by Guth, Wang and Zhang in 2019. In physics, OpenAI says it has proved the existence of certain quantum field theories in four dimensions, including a result relevant to Yang-Mills theory, one of the Clay Mathematics Institute’s Millennium Prize Problems.

Why This Matters

The news is less about any one result than about what it would mean if an AI system can reliably generate proofs that matter to working researchers. In each field, the claims would carry significant consequences. A proof that the ETH is false would shake theoretical computer science, because ETH underpins hundreds of papers across graph theory, databases, computational biology and computational geometry. Those papers would not necessarily become wrong, but their lower-bound guarantees would no longer be formally justified.

In mathematics, proving the local smoothing conjecture in all dimensions would also resolve two other prominent conjectures that follow from it: the Kakeya conjecture and Stein’s restriction conjecture. In physics, a proof that certain quantum field theories exist mathematically would not change the empirical success of the theories, but it would strengthen the formal foundations of a framework OpenAI calls central to modern theoretical physics. The company’s announcement therefore cuts to a larger question: can AI systems do more than summarise existing knowledge and begin to generate results that alter live research programmes?

How It Works

OpenAI’s verification process reflects the different standards of the three fields. For the computer science and mathematics results, the company translated the proofs into Lean so that other researchers can inspect the formal logic. That is important because formal proof systems reduce ambiguity: every step must be explicitly justified. But formal correctness is not the same as scientific acceptance. Experts still need to judge whether the assumptions, definitions and problem statements match the real question at issue.

The computer science result centres on SAT, the canonical example of a problem that is easy to check but hard to solve. Given a logical formula, the question is whether one can assign true/false values to its variables so that the whole formula becomes true. With a few variables, brute force is possible. But the number of possibilities doubles with each added variable, which is why SAT is considered computationally hard. ETH goes further than the more famous P versus NP question: it says there is no fundamentally faster algorithm for SAT in the worst case, not merely that the problem is hard in general. OpenAI’s claim would therefore not only challenge a widely held belief, but also the chain of assumptions built on top of it.

The physics result sits in the mathematical foundations of quantum field theory. OpenAI says it has proved the existence of certain interacting quantum field theories in four dimensions, organised in three layers: Yang-Mills theory, quantum chromodynamics and a grand unified theory. The crucial technical point is asymptotic freedom, which means the force becomes weaker at very short distances and keeps the mathematics manageable as one takes the lattice spacing toward zero. OpenAI explicitly says it has proved existence, not the mass gap required to solve the full Yang-Mills Millennium problem.

Implications and Future Directions

For now, the practical payoff remains uncertain. Ryan Williams, a computer scientist at MIT, said on X that nobody knows what everyday developers would notice first if the ETH result stands. He suggested it was plausible that practical SAT solving could see serious progress within 12 months — but added, “Or we may see nothing of the sort. I really don’t know.” That caution matters. SAT solvers are already highly optimised and routinely handle formulas with millions of variables in real applications such as chip verification and software testing. A breakthrough against the worst case would not automatically transform those systems.

The institutional response is also telling. Williams, Russell Impagliazzo and Ramamohan Paturi — the researchers who originally proposed the ETH with Francis Zane — will independently check and explain the result. They say they are doing so unpaid, without non-disclosure agreements, and plan to share interim findings as they go. Their open letter stresses that participation is not endorsement. OpenAI has also released the raw proof material for the computer science result, including the written paper and Lean proof, while committing to fund a conference on what the result means for the field. For the physics result, a separate shepherding group led by David Gross, who shared the 2004 Nobel Prize in Physics, will review the work, and OpenAI says it plans to let that group write up the result itself.

The different handling of each claim shows that the company is still searching for the right process. OpenAI itself acknowledges trade-offs: whoever selects the first shepherds influences who gets involved earliest, and some people inevitably learn more before others. It also says it is not claiming scientific credit for the discoveries; that should go to the researchers who explain and validate them. Those choices suggest an emerging reality for AI-assisted science: the bottleneck is no longer only generation, but verification, attribution and governance.

Expert Perspectives

The strongest expert response in the source material is procedural rather than celebratory. Williams and his collaborators explicitly say their role is not an endorsement, only a careful independent review. That point reflects a broader concern: in frontier mathematics and physics, an impressive-looking proof is not enough. If the statement is wrong, incomplete or formally mis-specified, the downstream consequences could be substantial. OpenAI’s own description of the manuscripts as “raw research data” underscores that uncertainty.

The Bigger Picture

This episode sits at the intersection of AI capability and scientific legitimacy. In AI safety terms, it highlights a subtler challenge than hallucination: even when a model produces something formally structured and apparently rigorous, the surrounding human process must still determine whether it is meaningful, original and valid. In the sciences, that means verification by specialists; in the public sphere, it means resisting the temptation to treat model-generated output as discovery before the community has checked it.

It also points to a future in which the most consequential AI outputs may not be chat responses or images, but candidate theorems, proofs and derivations. OpenAI’s release suggests that the research frontier is moving towards systems that can generate material requiring expert scrutiny rather than casual evaluation. Whether this becomes a genuine scientific revolution will depend less on the models’ brilliance than on the infrastructure around them: formal proof tools, independent review, transparent publication and clear standards for credit. For now, the central fact is simple enough — and astonishing enough: an AI has produced alleged proofs in fields where a single error can unravel the whole result, and the world’s experts are now being asked to decide what, if anything, has truly been proved.

How the brain may dodge catastrophic forgetting by giving each dendrite a different job

Artificial intelligence systems can be brittle: when they learn something new, older knowledge can vanish. A new modelling study suggests the brain avoids that trap not by storing memories in isolated neurons, but by letting different branches of the same neuron learn different things in different contexts. The result is a plausible answer to a long-standing neuroscience puzzle — and a fresh set of testable predictions about how memories are built and protected.

The Discovery/Development

The study, published in Neuron in August, was led by Julijana Gjorgjieva, a professor of computational neuroscience at the Technical University of Munich, together with Christoph Miehl, a postdoctoral fellow in Tim Vogels’ lab at the Institute of Science and Technology Austria, and colleagues. Rather than treating each neuron as a single unit of learning, the model gives each cell a soma and multiple dendritic compartments, each of which can independently generate NMDA-receptor-driven spikes and strengthen its connections.

In the simulation, inhibitory interneurons could selectively silence individual dendritic compartments, blocking plasticity there while leaving the rest of the neuron available for learning. By changing which inhibitory neurons were active, the model mimicked different contexts — for example, the way an animal’s brain may behave differently when it enters a new environment or internal state. Under those conditions, the network could form distinct neuronal assemblies, and those assemblies could overlap across brain areas without erasing earlier ones. As the article puts it, the work proposes “a realistic way for multiple, overlapping memories to form and coexist,” though the mechanism still needs experimental confirmation.

Why This Matters

The problem the study addresses is not just a technical headache for artificial intelligence. It is a central question in biology: how does the brain keep adding new information without overwriting what it already knows? In machine learning, catastrophic forgetting is a familiar failure mode. A system trained on new data can lose performance on old tasks because the very parameters that enabled previous learning are repurposed. The article notes that this same issue “plagues computational models of the brain” too, making the puzzle relevant across neuroscience and AI.

What makes the brain remarkable is that it appears to support both stability and flexibility at once. Memories must remain durable, yet learning must continue. The study suggests that one answer lies in the architecture of dendrites — the tree-like branches that extend from neurons and receive inputs. If different dendritic branches can act like semi-independent learning compartments, then a single neuron can participate in multiple memories without all those memories competing directly for the same plasticity machinery.

How It Works

The model is built around a few key ideas. First, it assumes that dendrites are not passive cables but local computational units. Second, it gives inhibitory neurons a very specific job: they can open learning in one dendritic compartment while keeping others locked. Third, it lets context signals determine which inhibitory neurons are turned down, effectively choosing which parts of the neuron are available for plasticity.

That arrangement creates a form of selective learning. In the simulation, groups of excitatory neurons activated the network much as sensory input might in the brain. Each group formed a different assembly — a functional cluster representing a thought or memory. Crucially, if the same excitatory group was activated again in a different context, it could form a separate assembly rather than overwrite the earlier one. And when only part of an original pattern reappeared, the full assembly could be reactivated — but only if the context matched the one in which that memory had first formed.

The model also extends across brain areas. The researchers simulated multiple recurrently connected networks linked by feedforward connections. In that setup, one area could send a copy of an assembly to another. If two different upstream areas both projected to a shared downstream area under the same context, the downstream area merged the patterns into a single joint representation. Miehl says this could resemble combining information from different senses — “binding an image and sound together into a single concept.”

The study also offers concrete predictions. Attila Losonczy, a professor of neuroscience at the University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center, says the model implies that distinct subgroups within inhibitory cell classes should respond to distinct contextual signals, target specific dendritic branches and be associated with systematic shifts in dendritic activation. He adds that his own recent findings support the idea that different parts of the dendrite make different computations. A separate paper in Science Advances reported tiny constrictions in hippocampal and cortical dendrites that could physically partition them into compartments — anatomical support, as Gjorgjieva puts it, for “the broader idea that dendrites contain local computational and plasticity compartments.”

Still, the model is intentionally simplified. Sebastian Onasch, a former graduate student in Gjorgjieva’s lab, notes that it assumes the inhibitory population connects to only one dendrite of each neuron in a given context. Real inhibitory wiring is likely more complex. Gjorgjieva says the mechanism does not require that exact assumption to be informative, but that without context-dependent inhibition the network loses some of its ability to segregate stimuli. The model is therefore best understood as a proof of principle — a biologically motivated map of what might be happening, not a final answer.

Expert Perspectives

The paper has drawn strong praise from theorists. Christos Papadimitriou, a computer scientist at Columbia University, calls it “a major advance” towards explaining how the spiking of individual neurons gives rise to complex behaviour. He argues that neuronal assemblies, rather than single neurons, are increasingly understood as the brain’s basic computational units, and says his own earlier theory of assemblies is now “biologically realised” by this work. “I was very impressed with the thoroughness and how imaginative [the study] is,” he says. “It’s something that I was hoping somebody would do.”

Losonczy is similarly enthusiastic about the model’s predictive power. “The study provides us with a novel function of dendritic inhibition,” he says. “It provides a really strong testable hypothesis of what comes next.”

The Bigger Picture

For neuroscience, the study strengthens a broader shift away from thinking of neurons as simple on/off devices. The emerging picture is messier and more interesting: dendrites can compute locally, inhibition can be spatially specific, and context can determine not just whether learning occurs, but where inside a neuron it occurs. That kind of compartmentalised learning may help explain how brains maintain memory stability while remaining adaptable — a problem that artificial neural networks still handle poorly.

For AI, the parallels are obvious but not one-to-one. The brain’s solution, if confirmed, would suggest that continual learning might require more than better optimisation tricks or regularisation. It may require architecture: distinct subcomponents, context-sensitive gating and localised plasticity. The question now is whether those biological principles can be demonstrated in living circuits. Miehl says the biggest open issue is what “context” actually is in the brain, and where it comes from. That question points to the next stage of the work: moving beyond a visual-auditory toy task towards a reward-based behavioural experiment a mouse might actually solve. Until then, the study offers something valuable in neuroscience and in AI alike — a testable theory of how new learning can coexist with old memory instead of erasing it.

Grammatik: Passiv mit Modalverben

Today’s grammar point is the Passiv mit Modalverben, which is very useful when you want to focus on what must, can, or should be done, not on who does it. In the article, the important idea is that outside experts will check the AI’s proofs. In German, you can often express this with müssen / können / sollen + Passiv. Pattern: Modalverb + Partizip II + werden Examples: - Die Beweise müssen geprüft werden. (The proofs must be checked.) - Die Ergebnisse können später veröffentlicht werden. (The results can be published later.) - Die Texte sollen von Expertinnen kontrolliert werden. (The texts are supposed to be checked by experts.) Why use it? It sounds formal and is common in news, science, and official language. It is especially helpful when the person doing the action is unknown, unimportant, or obvious. Extra example: Der Bericht muss noch einmal gelesen werden. (The report still has to be read again.)

Grammatik - Setze die richtigen Verbformen im Passiv mit Modalverben ein. Achte auf die Endstellung des Partizips und auf das Verb werden.

  1. Die Beweise müssen von unabhängigen Fachleuten ___ werden.
  2. Die Ergebnisse können erst nach der Prüfung ___ werden.
  3. Die Manuskripte sollen sehr genau ___ werden.
  4. Neue Daten dürfen nicht ohne Kontrolle ___ werden.

Kultureller Fakt (Cultural Fact)

Warum Deutsch in der Wissenschaft oft sehr genau klingt

In German media and academic writing, passive forms are very common because they sound precise, neutral, and formal. That is why news about research often says what must be checked, tested, or confirmed instead of constantly naming the people doing it.
