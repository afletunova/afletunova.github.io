Chips for Humanity: The Quiet Infrastructure Question Behind AI Safety

AI safety research is often discussed as a question of ideas, benchmarks and theory. But the LessWrong essay “Chips for Humanity: Compute for AI safety research” argues that a far more basic constraint may shape the future of the field: who can access enough compute to do the work at all, and how quickly that access can be organised when frontier systems become more capable.

The Discovery/Development

The post makes a policy and infrastructure case for treating compute as a strategic resource for AI safety research. Its central claim is not that a single technical breakthrough has been made, but that a practical bottleneck is becoming increasingly important: safety researchers need reliable access to chips, GPUs and cloud-scale compute in order to evaluate models, run experiments, replicate results and probe dangerous capabilities. If the field cannot secure that capacity in advance, then even well-designed safety agendas could stall simply because the necessary hardware is too scarce, too expensive or too slow to obtain when it matters most.

Rather than framing the issue as an abstract resource debate, the article presents compute access as a concrete enabling condition for research. In other words, the question is not whether safety work is valuable in principle, but whether institutions will be able to buy or allocate enough compute to let that work happen at the speed frontier AI development demands.

Why This Matters

This matters because AI safety work is increasingly entangled with the same infrastructure that powers frontier model development. The more capable systems become, the more expensive and computationally intensive it is to study them properly. Safety teams may need to run large-scale evaluations, carry out adversarial testing, explore failure modes across many model variants, and iterate rapidly as new systems are released. Those tasks are not cheap, and they are not easy to improvise after the fact.

The article’s wider implication is that compute scarcity is not merely a budgeting problem. It is a governance problem. If safety researchers cannot obtain timely access to chips and compute, then the research agenda may skew towards the projects that are cheapest to run rather than the ones most relevant to managing advanced AI systems. That could leave the field underprepared precisely when the stakes rise.

How It Works

The essay’s argument rests on a simple but important premise: modern AI safety research often depends on the same class of computational resources used to train and deploy advanced models. Chips, especially high-end accelerators, are needed not only for training large systems but also for running repeated experiments, stress-testing behaviours, and analysing model performance at scale. When compute is abundant, researchers can test more hypotheses, compare more interventions and iterate faster. When it is scarce, research becomes narrower, slower and more dependent on whatever resources happen to be available.

That creates a structural vulnerability. Safety work cannot always wait until a problem is obvious to the wider public; by then, the relevant models may already be more capable, more widely deployed and harder to scrutinise. The article therefore treats compute access as part of preparedness: an infrastructural capacity that should be arranged before the pressure peak arrives, not after.

It is also important that the essay focuses on access, not only on total global supply. Even if the world produces more chips over time, that does not automatically mean safety researchers will be able to use them. Allocation, procurement, institutional priority and logistics all matter. A research group with a compelling safety agenda can still be constrained if it lacks purchasing power, cloud credits, supply-chain access or the organisational relationships needed to secure large-scale compute.

Implications and Future Directions

The most immediate implication is that AI safety may need a more explicit infrastructure strategy. That could mean building compute reserves for safety work, establishing purchasing arrangements in advance, or creating institutions that can allocate hardware to researchers working on priority safety questions. The article suggests that planning for this now is preferable to scrambling later, when chips may be more contested, more expensive and harder to redirect.

There is also a broader research implication: access to compute may shape what kinds of safety questions are studied at all. Large-scale empirical work is often more persuasive than small-scale speculation, but it requires resources. If compute remains concentrated in a handful of powerful labs, then independent safety research may struggle to keep pace with frontier development. That, in turn, could reduce the diversity of scrutiny applied to advanced systems.

The essay also points to a timing challenge. Safety institutions must anticipate future demand before capability advances make that demand acute. That is difficult because it requires planning for an uncertain future: how much compute will be needed, which projects will matter most, and what form of access will be most useful. The article does not present an easy formula, but it does insist that these questions are too important to leave until the last minute.

Expert Perspectives

The source material is presented as a reasoning piece rather than a report of interviews or a survey of outside experts. As a result, it does not offer competing quoted viewpoints or named reactions. Its force comes instead from connecting a familiar strategic insight — that infrastructure shapes outcomes — to the specific case of AI safety research.

The Bigger Picture

What makes this argument consequential is that it widens the lens on AI safety. The public conversation often focuses on model alignment, interpretability, regulation or international competition. Those issues remain vital, but the article reminds readers that research capacity is itself a part of the safety stack. If the field lacks the computational means to investigate dangerous systems, then even the best theories may remain untested.

There is a deeper theme here about the political economy of frontier AI. In many emerging technologies, the ability to study a system is inseparable from the ability to build and scale it. AI is unusually stark in this respect: the actors with the most compute are often the actors best positioned to define the research agenda. The article’s warning is that without deliberate efforts to create compute access for safety work, the field may inherit the priorities of whoever controls the hardware.

Seen that way, “Chips for Humanity” is less about machines than about power: who gets to ask hard questions, who gets to answer them, and whether the people trying to make AI safer will have the material means to do so before the next generation of systems arrives.

One splicing fix revives failing synapses in a key ALS and dementia pathway

Scientists have shown that correcting a single mis-splicing event in the gene UNC13A can rescue synaptic dysfunction caused by the loss of TDP-43, a protein central to amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and many cases of frontotemporal dementia. In human neurons, the intervention restored neurotransmitter release and network synchrony — evidence that a precise RNA repair can reverse a major functional defect before neurons visibly degenerate.

The Discovery/Development

The study, published in Nature, addresses a long-standing question in neurodegeneration: when TDP-43 disappears from the nucleus, do neurons merely become molecularly abnormal, or do they lose core physiological functions as well? The answer here is striking. The authors show that TDP-43 depletion in mature human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived neurons causes a pronounced breakdown in synaptic communication, even while the cells remain structurally intact. Crucially, when they genetically corrected one TDP-43-sensitive cryptic splicing event in UNC13A, much of that synaptic failure was reversed.

To reach that result, the team used several complementary systems. First, they created human neurons in which endogenous TDP-43 could be depleted on demand using a HaloTag/proteolysis-targeting chimera approach. They then measured synaptic behaviour with whole-cell patch clamp recordings and multi-electrode arrays (MEAs), which capture both individual synaptic events and broader network activity. They also used CRISPR–Cas9 to delete the UNC13A cryptic exon from the genome, allowing them to test whether that single splicing defect was driving the dysfunction. In a separate humanised mouse model, they showed that splice-switching antisense oligonucleotides could correct the same cryptic exon inclusion event.

Why This Matters

TDP-43 pathology sits at the centre of most ALS cases outside the rare SOD1 and FUS genetic subtypes, and is also seen in about half of frontotemporal dementia cases, according to the paper. Yet there are still no therapies that directly target TDP-43’s nuclear loss of function. That gap matters because TDP-43 is not a minor accessory protein: it is an RNA-binding regulator with widespread control over splicing. When it mislocalises from the nucleus to the cytoplasm, hundreds of transcripts are affected.

UNC13A has emerged as one of the most important of those transcripts. It encodes a presynaptic protein required to dock and prime synaptic vesicles for release. Previous genetic studies linked risk variants near a cryptic exon in UNC13A to ALS risk and progression, but the new paper goes beyond association. It shows that the mis-splicing event is not just a biomarker of disease biology; it can be a causal driver of synaptic failure. That makes it a concrete therapeutic target in a disease landscape where many interventions have struggled to move from molecular logic to functional benefit.

The broader implication is also conceptual. Neurodegeneration often unfolds in phases: molecular dysfunction first, then circuit failure, then cell death. This work suggests there may be a predegenerative window in which neurons are still alive and morphologically preserved, but synapses are already malfunctioning. That is the kind of stage at which intervention may be most meaningful.

How It Works

The mechanism centres on alternative splicing. Under normal conditions, TDP-43 helps suppress a cryptic exon embedded in UNC13A. When TDP-43 is lost from the nucleus, that cryptic exon is included in the mature RNA. The result is reduced correctly spliced UNC13A transcript and, consequently, less UNC13A protein at synapses.

In the experiments, TDP-43 depletion caused a sharp drop in UNC13A protein over time — to less than 5% after 10 days in one model — and immunostaining showed loss of UNC13A at synapsin-positive presynaptic puncta. Functionally, the neurons fired fewer miniature excitatory postsynaptic currents, a sign that synaptic vesicle release was impaired. The deficit was presynaptic rather than postsynaptic: the amplitude of those currents was largely unchanged when UNC13A alone was depleted, which fits UNC13A’s role in vesicle priming rather than receptor function.

The strongest evidence came from the rescue experiments. By deleting the cryptic exon from the genome, the researchers restored correct UNC13A splicing, recovered protein levels, and reversed much of the synaptic impairment after TDP-43 loss. In human neurons, this produced about 80% recovery in miniature excitatory current frequency. Network-level synchrony on MEA recordings also improved, suggesting that the rescued synapses were not merely detectable under the microscope, but functionally capable of supporting coordinated signalling across neuronal ensembles.

Implications and Future Directions

The most immediate implication is therapeutic. The authors argue that UNC13A splice correction could be relevant to roughly 97% of ALS patients and about 50% of frontotemporal dementia patients with TDP-43 pathology. That is a substantial potential reach compared with therapies limited to rare mutation-defined subgroups. It also broadens the therapeutic imagination for ALS: rather than trying to replace or suppress TDP-43 directly, one can intervene downstream at a defined pathological consequence of its loss.

But the study also defines the limits of a single-target repair. TDP-43 regulates hundreds of transcripts, and the authors acknowledge that their correction of UNC13A did not fully normalise every readout. For example, TDP-43 depletion caused a modest reduction in synaptic current amplitude and a reduction in synapse number that was not completely reversed by cryptic exon deletion. That means UNC13A rescue appears to restore a major component of synaptic dysfunction, not the entire disease phenotype.

That nuance matters for drug development. The data support UNC13A as a validated target, but not necessarily as a complete solution to TDP-43 proteinopathy. Future work will need to determine how much benefit splice correction can deliver in vivo, how early treatment must begin, and whether similar RNA-level rescue strategies can be built for other TDP-43-sensitive genes such as STMN2 or KCNQ2. The paper also relies heavily on human cell models and a humanised mouse system; that is a strength for biological relevance, but it still leaves open questions about long-term efficacy, delivery, dosing, and safety in patients.

The Bigger Picture

This study sits within a broader shift in neuroscience from treating neurodegeneration as a single catastrophic event to understanding it as a sequence of molecularly traceable failures. It also reflects a growing confidence in RNA therapeutics: not only can antisense molecules modulate splicing, they can do so with enough precision to restore a physiologically meaningful function. That is important in diseases where protein aggregates, mis-splicing and circuit dysfunction are tightly intertwined.

There is also a useful lesson here for the wider biomedical field, including AI-enabled drug discovery efforts. The most promising interventions are often those grounded in precise causal chains rather than broad correlation. This paper connects a disease hallmark — TDP-43 nuclear loss — to a defined RNA defect, to a presynaptic protein deficit, to measurable network failure, and then back to rescue by targeted correction. That kind of end-to-end mechanistic map is exactly what translational neuroscience has often lacked.

For ALS and frontotemporal dementia research, the message is both sobering and hopeful. TDP-43 pathology remains a formidable problem, but this study shows that at least one of its most damaging consequences can be reversed. In a field long accustomed to incremental biomarkers and elusive targets, that is a notable step from explanation towards intervention.
