A Genetic Switch May Have Helped Primate Brains Grow Bigger Without Inventing New Genes

The human cerebral cortex did not necessarily need a completely new genetic toolkit to become large and elaborate. According to a new study, primate evolution appears to have worked by rewiring the control system of an ancient gene, helping the brain produce more of the specialised stem cells that build the cortex.

The Discovery/Development

Researchers from National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University, Academia Sinica and Kyoto University have identified a primate-specific regulatory DNA element that controls DACH1, a developmental gene shared by mice, monkeys and humans. The study, reported in Science Advances, suggests that the evolutionary expansion of the primate neocortex depended not on inventing a new gene, but on altering how an existing gene is switched on during brain development.

The team focused on basal radial glia, or bRG, a class of neural stem cells that is abundant in primates and scarce in rodents. These cells are central to cortical growth because they self-renew and generate large numbers of neurons, helping enlarge the outer brain layer responsible for language, memory, perception and abstract reasoning. By comparing human, monkey and mouse systems, the researchers found that a small noncoding DNA sequence acts as an enhancer-like switch for DACH1 in primates.

The evidence came from several directions. In human and rhesus monkey cortical organoids, deleting the primate regulatory region sharply reduced bRG marker-positive cells. In a separate gain-of-function experiment, inserting the human regulatory element into developing mouse brains increased the number of cells with bRG-like features. Live imaging of dividing neural stem cells also showed that boosting DACH1 activity increased the share of daughter cells becoming bRG-like progenitors from about 4% to nearly 15%.

Why This Matters

For decades, evolutionary neurobiologists have debated how primates, especially humans, developed such an enlarged and folded cortex. One leading idea has been that brain expansion required new genes or entirely new cellular programmes. This study points to a subtler mechanism: evolution can generate major anatomical change by revising the regulatory instructions that govern when and where familiar genes are used.

That matters because the cortex is not merely larger in primates; it is organised differently, with a greater capacity for producing upper-layer neurons and long-range connections between cortical regions and across the two hemispheres. In the authors’ framing, bRG act like a high-capacity workforce in a biological factory. Expand that workforce, and the factory can make far more of the cells needed for a complex cerebral cortex.

The finding also helps explain why mice, despite sharing many developmental genes with primates, build much smaller and less folded cortices. The difference may lie less in the gene list than in the regulatory logic controlling that list.

How It Works

The study centres on a developmental pathway involving LHX2, DACH1 and miR-215-5p. According to the paper’s abstract, DACH1 is enriched in human neurogenic radial glia, and it promotes bRG generation by suppressing miR-215-5p–mediated down-regulation of genes related to centromeres and centrosomes. In plain terms, DACH1 helps preserve a stem-cell state that favours bRG production rather than pushing progenitors into less flexible developmental routes.

The regulatory innovation itself is a primate-evolved enhancer controlling DACH1. When the researchers removed this enhancer from primate organoid systems, bRG production fell. When they inserted it into mice, bRG-like cells increased. The paper also reports that DACH1 loss in human cortical progenitors impaired bRG production and shifted neuronal output towards corticospinal neurons, whereas Dach1 depletion in mice had no effect. Conversely, ectopic DACH1 expression in the rodent neocortex induced bRG formation and promoted callosal neuron production.

This matters because neuronal subtype balance shapes cortical wiring. The study reports that the DACH1 pathway shifts output towards neurons that help connect the brain’s hemispheres, offering a possible developmental route toward the primate cortical connectome.

Implications and Future Directions

The researchers are careful not to overstate the finding as a single-gene explanation for human intelligence. Brain evolution, they note, involves complex multi-gene networks rather than one master switch. Even so, the study offers a concrete example of how a noncoding DNA change can reshape developmental cell fate and, by extension, brain architecture.

The remaining question is not whether this enhancer matters — the experiments suggest that it does — but how broadly this mechanism fits into the larger evolutionary story. The paper points to one important route by which the primate brain became large and intricate; it does not claim to be the only one.

The Bigger Picture

This study is a reminder that complexity in biology often emerges from control systems, not just components. A genome can stay largely conserved while its regulatory circuitry changes enough to produce a dramatically different organ. In that sense, the primate brain may exemplify a general rule relevant far beyond neuroscience: small changes in instructions can produce large changes in output.

That idea resonates with a broader theme in neuroscience and in AI: structure and behaviour depend heavily on how systems are trained, regulated and constrained. In the brain, as in machine learning systems, the architecture may matter less than the rules governing how the parts are used. Here, evolution appears to have tuned those rules to bias development towards a larger cortical workforce.

For readers interested in human origins, the message is elegant and unsettling at once. Our expanded cortex may owe part of its rise not to a dramatic burst of genetic novelty, but to a quiet rewrite in the regulatory margins of the genome.
