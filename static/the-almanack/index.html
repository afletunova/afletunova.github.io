Who Should AI Listen to First? A New Framework for Fairer Preference Learning

When AI systems are trained to follow human preferences, the usual approach is simple: gather feedback and average it. But what happens when people disagree, and the training budget is too small to hear everyone equally? A new paper on welfare-aware preference acquisition argues that alignment systems should not merely collect more feedback — they should decide whose feedback matters most in a way that reflects both disagreement and impact.

The Discovery/Development

The paper, Who Should We Listen to More? Welfare-Aware Preference Acquisition for Pluralistic Alignment, tackles a problem that sits at the heart of modern alignment work: human preferences are not uniform, and training data for AI preference models is always limited. Rather than treating all feedback as interchangeable, the authors propose a framework for deciding which people, or which kinds of preferences, should be prioritised when acquiring new preference data for model training.

The central idea is that if an alignment system is meant to serve a pluralistic population, then the acquisition process itself should be welfare-aware. In practical terms, this means the system should choose which human judgments to sample with an eye towards improving outcomes for the people who would be most affected by the resulting model behaviour, rather than simply maximising generic prediction accuracy over an undifferentiated crowd.

Why This Matters

AI labs increasingly rely on preference learning — including methods that use human rankings, comparisons, or approvals — to make models more helpful, harmless, and usable. Yet real users do not share identical values. One group may care most about factualness, another about politeness, another about avoiding offence, and another about preserving minority perspectives. If training data is collected indiscriminately, the resulting model can end up over-representing the preferences of the easiest-to-reach or most frequently consulted users.

This paper matters because it reframes data collection as an alignment decision, not just a logistics problem. In settings where feedback is expensive, scarce, or unevenly distributed, the question is no longer only “what do people prefer?” but “whose preferences should we ask about first?” That shift has direct implications for how future systems are trained, especially in domains where model decisions can affect different groups in unequal ways.

How It Works

At a high level, the framework asks how to allocate limited human feedback so that preference acquisition better serves overall welfare under disagreement. Instead of assuming a single consensus objective, the approach recognises that alignment must operate in a pluralistic setting: multiple legitimate preference distributions exist, and the training process should account for that diversity.

The paper’s contribution is not simply to note that preferences differ, but to formalise the acquisition problem around that fact. In standard preference learning, one might select queries to reduce uncertainty or improve model fit. Here, the selection criterion is more normatively loaded: it asks which additional labels are most valuable for improving the model in ways that matter for human well-being. That can mean prioritising data from individuals whose interests are currently under-served, or from situations where the cost of getting the preference model wrong would be especially high.

In other words, the paper treats feedback not as a neutral commodity but as a scarce resource whose distribution has moral consequences. The technical novelty lies in embedding welfare considerations directly into the data acquisition strategy, thereby linking active learning-style selection methods with alignment goals. The resulting system is designed to be more sensitive to the social structure of disagreement, rather than pretending that all preferences can be collapsed into one average target.

Implications and Future Directions

If this line of work develops successfully, it could influence how AI developers choose annotators, design user studies, and build preference models for deployment. Instead of asking only how to minimise annotation cost, teams may need to ask whether their sampling process systematically privileges some users over others. For frontier AI systems deployed at scale, that could become a serious governance issue: the groups that are easiest to measure may not be the groups most important to protect.

The broader implication is that alignment research may need to become more explicitly distributive. A model trained to satisfy an “average” user can hide large welfare losses among minority groups or edge cases. Welfare-aware acquisition offers a way to surface those trade-offs earlier, at the level of data collection rather than after deployment. It also points towards a future in which alignment metrics may need to reflect not just performance, but fairness in the acquisition of the very data that shapes performance.

At the same time, the approach raises difficult questions. How should “welfare” be defined when preferences conflict? Which stakeholders get counted, and how should their interests be weighted? And who decides when it is justified to prioritise one group’s feedback over another’s? The paper’s framework makes these questions unavoidable, but it does not eliminate them. Instead, it shows that any serious attempt at pluralistic alignment must confront them head-on.

The Bigger Picture

The paper fits into a wider shift in AI safety: from assuming one universally correct objective to managing systems that must operate amid value diversity, uncertainty, and contestation. That is a meaningful change. Earlier alignment work often focused on eliciting a single “true” preference or reward signal. But as AI systems become more socially embedded, the challenge increasingly looks less like recovering a hidden scalar value and more like governing disagreement fairly.

That broader challenge resonates beyond AI. In politics, medicine, and public policy, institutions routinely face the problem of deciding whose preferences, risks, and benefits receive priority under resource constraints. The paper brings that problem into the design of AI training pipelines. Its message is consequential but sober: if model behaviour is shaped by human feedback, then the ethics of which humans are asked, when, and for what purpose become part of alignment itself.

Scientists may have overlooked a hidden Alzheimer’s culprit for decades

For years, Alzheimer’s research has revolved around amyloid beta, the sticky protein fragment long blamed for the disease’s destructive brain changes. Now a University of California, Santa Cruz biochemist argues that scientists may have missed a closely related peptide, P3, that can also clump, may also harm neurons, and could help explain why so many amyloid-targeting therapies have disappointed.

The Discovery/Development

The new argument comes from UC Santa Cruz professor Jevgenij Raskatov, whose commentary in ChemBioChem highlights evidence that the peptide known as P3 is not the harmless bystander many researchers assumed it to be. P3 is generated from the amyloid precursor protein, or APP, through a different enzymatic route from amyloid beta: α-secretase acts first, then γ-secretase. For years, scientists believed that this pathway produced a soluble, non-toxic fragment that would not form the harmful aggregates associated with Alzheimer’s disease.

Raskatov says that assumption was never adequately tested. Over the past five years, his laboratory has published three major studies indicating that P3 can form microscopic clumps and threadlike fibrils — the same broad kind of amyloid structures associated with disease — and can do so even faster than amyloid beta. The team also found evidence that P3 may itself be harmful to neurons, though apparently less toxic than amyloid beta. According to the article, an independent group in the United Kingdom has since confirmed and extended those findings.

Why This Matters

The stakes are considerable because Alzheimer’s is the world’s most common neurodegenerative disease, affecting about 35 million people and generating more than $800 billion in annual costs, according to the source material. The number of people living with the disease is expected to double by 2050. Yet despite more than 400 clinical trials, progress has been painfully slow, with many amyloid-focused drugs failing outright and others offering only modest benefit.

That long record of disappointment has forced researchers to ask whether the field has been looking too narrowly at amyloid beta alone. If P3 is also aggregating in the brain, and if it interacts with amyloid beta in ways that influence accumulation or toxicity, then some of the central assumptions that shaped decades of drug development may need revisiting. Raskatov says the current state of therapy “leaves much to be desired” and that “fundamentally new approaches” are needed.

How It Works

To understand the importance of P3, it helps to know how APP is processed. Amyloid beta is produced when APP is cut first by β-secretase and then by γ-secretase. Researchers have focused especially on two versions, Aβ40 and Aβ42, because the latter is particularly prone to sticking together into toxic aggregates. That biological tendency helped make amyloid beta the central villain in Alzheimer’s research.

P3 comes from a separate cleavage sequence. Instead of β-secretase beginning the process, α-secretase cuts APP first, followed by γ-secretase. The resulting peptide was long assumed to be chemically bland: soluble, non-aggregating and not toxic. Raskatov’s work challenges all three assumptions. His team reports that P3 can form amyloid deposits at least as readily as amyloid beta, and in some experiments more rapidly. The peptide may also interact with amyloid beta, potentially changing how both molecules build up and how damaging they become.

Raskatov has even proposed a different name for the molecule — “Amyloid α”, or Aα — to reflect the possibility that it belongs in the amyloid family rather than outside it. That naming proposal is more than semantic. If accepted, it would signal a conceptual shift in how the field thinks about Alzheimer’s chemistry: not a single-protein problem, but a network of interacting aggregation pathways.

Implications and Future Directions

If P3 proves relevant in patients, the implications could reach far beyond one overlooked peptide. First, it could help explain why therapies aimed solely at amyloid beta have not been more effective. Clearing one species may not be enough if another related fragment is still aggregating, interacting, or seeding toxicity. Second, it could broaden the search for biomarkers and drug targets beyond the familiar Aβ40 and Aβ42 pathways.

There is also a cautionary lesson here about scientific consensus. Raskatov says he identified at least four peer-reviewed papers that cited his group’s work as evidence that P3 was harmless and incapable of forming amyloid deposits — the opposite of what his experiments showed. That kind of citation error, if widespread, can harden an untested assumption into orthodoxy and steer an entire field away from potentially important biology.

For now, however, the article stops short of claiming that P3 has been proven to drive Alzheimer’s in patients. The evidence described is compelling enough to warrant attention, but the source itself notes that further research is needed to establish whether and how P3 contributes to disease in people. The key next questions are clinical: Is P3 present in relevant amounts in the human brain? Does it co-aggregate with amyloid beta in living tissue? And if so, does it change disease progression or treatment response?

Expert Perspectives

Support for a broader re-evaluation comes from David Teplow, an emeritus professor of neurology at UCLA and a prominent Alzheimer’s researcher, who reviewed Raskatov’s work independently. Teplow said amyloid beta has long been regarded as a central cause of Alzheimer’s, but argued that this reassessment “has far-reaching consequences for both basic science and clinical research into the causes and treatment of Alzheimer’s disease.”

That view matters because it suggests the field is not merely adding another peptide to an already crowded list of suspects. It is reconsidering the architecture of the disease itself — what counts as a causal amyloid species, which molecular interactions matter most, and how narrowly the last several decades of research may have defined the problem.

The Bigger Picture

The story of P3 is a reminder of how neuroscience advances: not only through dramatic breakthroughs, but through the correction of long-standing blind spots. In Alzheimer’s research, a dominant model can be both productive and limiting. It can generate therapies, diagnostics and hypotheses — but it can also cause the field to overlook molecules that do not fit the prevailing story.

That lesson extends beyond Alzheimer’s. In neuroscience, as in other fields, complex disorders often emerge from interacting biological systems rather than a single molecular culprit. The P3 debate therefore speaks to a broader scientific challenge: how to keep a successful framework open to revision when new data suggest the disease is more complicated than expected. In this case, the overlooked fragment may not simply add detail to the amyloid hypothesis. It may force researchers to redraw its boundaries.
