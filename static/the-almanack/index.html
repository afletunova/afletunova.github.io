Gemini 4 Carbon wins the math crown — but its lead comes with an eye-watering price tag

Google’s Gemini 4 Carbon has climbed to the top of the MathArena benchmark, overtaking OpenAI’s GPT-6.1 Sol and becoming the first non-OpenAI model in some time to lead the test. But the result comes with a stark caveat: in the reasoning settings used for evaluation, Carbon costs roughly 15 times as much as GPT-6.1 Sol, raising a familiar question in advanced AI — is better performance worth the bill?

The Discovery/Development

According to a benchmark summary shared by Jasper Dekoninck on X and reported by The Decoder, Gemini 4 Carbon, in its “high” setting, scored first on MathArena with an expected performance of 85.4% ±3.0%. That put it ahead of Anthropic’s Claude-Fable-5.2 (82.8% ±5.3%) and OpenAI’s GPT-6.1 Sol (80.6% ±2.7%).

The ranking is notable not just because Google took the top spot, but because OpenAI models have dominated recent public discussion around elite reasoning systems. The article also notes that OpenAI’s most recent models — including ones said to be tackling demanding equations such as the Navier-Stokes equations and the Hodge conjecture — are not publicly available yet, making the benchmark one of the few visible comparative windows into the state of the art.

Why This Matters

Math benchmarks matter because they are often treated as proxies for a model’s ability to reason carefully, persist through multi-step problems, and avoid brittle mistakes. In practice, success on these tests can influence which models enterprises choose for high-stakes use cases ranging from research support to technical analysis. A model that leads on a respected benchmark can therefore carry real market weight — especially when the task is symbolic reasoning rather than casual conversation.

But this result also highlights a growing tension in AI development: performance gains increasingly arrive with steep computational costs. On the figures reported here, Gemini 4 Carbon’s lead over GPT-6.1 Sol comes at roughly 15 times the price, while Anthropic’s Claude-Fable-5.2 is said to cost around 40 times as much as GPT-6.1 Sol under the same testing conditions. That gap matters because cost is not a side issue; it shapes whether a model can be deployed broadly, iterated on quickly, or reserved for elite workflows only.

How It Works

The benchmark numbers reflect models run with particular reasoning settings, which is important: these systems can be tuned to spend more time thinking, checking, and exploring candidate solutions. In other words, the headline scores are not simply a measure of raw model ability, but of ability under a specific level of deliberation. That distinction helps explain why a model can outperform rivals on accuracy while still being dramatically more expensive to operate.

The article flags a key characteristic of Gemini 4 Carbon in this setting: its most common failure mode is exceeding the time limit. That suggests the model often keeps working rather than stopping early, and the piece notes that it “almost never gives up on a task.” In one striking example, it reportedly launched a script that ran for more than eleven hours without checking its progress. Depending on one’s perspective, that persistence is either a sign of useful determination or a warning sign of inefficient, poorly bounded behaviour.

This matters because advanced reasoning models are increasingly judged not only by whether they arrive at the right answer, but by how they manage the process of getting there. A system that persistently explores may solve more hard problems — yet it may also spend vast amounts of compute chasing marginal gains, with obvious implications for latency, cost and reliability.

Implications and Future Directions

The immediate implication is that benchmark leadership does not automatically translate into practical dominance. A model that is 4.8 percentage points ahead of GPT-6.1 Sol on the reported MathArena score may look superior on paper, but the operational burden is substantial. For organisations thinking in terms of total cost of ownership — not just accuracy — a 15-fold price premium can erase much of the appeal.

The pricing spread also suggests a wider strategic divide in the market. Some labs may pursue maximum benchmark performance even when the marginal gains are expensive, betting that the prestige of being “best” will justify the spend. Others may optimise for efficiency, aiming for a more deployable model that is only slightly behind on score but vastly cheaper to run. Claude-Fable-5.2’s position in the table, with strong performance but even higher reported cost than Carbon, illustrates how quickly the frontier can become economically strained.

The Bigger Picture

The MathArena result reflects a broader trend in frontier AI: capability gains are increasingly tied to infrastructure, inference strategy and willingness to spend compute at run time. That has clear implications for AI safety as well as product design. If a model’s behaviour changes significantly when given more time to reason, then controlling when and how long it reasons becomes part of the safety and governance problem — not merely an engineering detail.

The article’s note that Gemini 4 Carbon “almost never gives up” points to this tension. Persistence can improve problem-solving, but it can also produce runaway processes that are hard to supervise. In that sense, the benchmark is a small example of a larger issue in advanced AI: the systems we build are becoming more capable of extended autonomous work, yet the cost and control trade-offs are becoming harder to ignore.

Alzheimer’s may be visible in the brain a decade before memory loss begins

A Johns Hopkins University study suggests Alzheimer’s disease may start eroding two brain regions long before the hippocampus shows the changes doctors traditionally look for. By tracking hundreds of MRI and PET scans, researchers found that the entorhinal cortex and amygdala begin shrinking earlier — a pattern linked to abnormal tau protein and potentially detectable up to 10 years before symptoms appear.

The Discovery/Development

Researchers at Johns Hopkins University report that Alzheimer’s-related brain atrophy may first show up in the entorhinal cortex and amygdala, rather than in the hippocampus, which has long been a central reference point in the disease. The team analysed hundreds of brain scans from two independent research efforts: the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative and the Biomarkers of Cognitive Decline Among Normal Individuals study. The scans followed people across the Alzheimer’s spectrum, including those without symptoms, those with cognitive impairment, and patients who later died from the disease.

Across both datasets, the same pattern emerged: the entorhinal cortex and amygdala showed greater early tissue loss than the hippocampus. The study, published in NeuroImage: Reports, was also presented at Neuroscience 2026, the annual meeting of the Society for Neuroscience.

Why This Matters

Alzheimer’s disease is the most common cause of dementia worldwide, but it does not begin when memory problems become obvious. Its biological changes can accumulate for decades before a person starts forgetting names, appointments or familiar routes. That long silent period is precisely what makes the new findings important: if doctors can spot structural changes earlier, there may be a much wider window in which treatment could slow damage before it becomes entrenched.

“We have found that there are early changes in the brain manifesting the clinical biomarkers of atrophy as much as a decade before there are clinical symptoms,” said senior author Michael Miller, a biomedical engineer at Johns Hopkins. He added that the critical promise lies in moving treatment forward by years, rather than waiting until memory symptoms are fully established. Co-author Marilyn Albert, director of the Johns Hopkins Alzheimer’s Disease Research Center, framed the goal plainly: to treat people before symptoms develop so the disease may be delayed, or even stopped.

How It Works

The hippocampus has traditionally been the star of Alzheimer’s imaging, because it is central to memory and is often affected as the disease advances. But the Johns Hopkins team found that two nearby structures may offer earlier warning signals. The entorhinal cortex helps retrieve previously stored memories, while the amygdala helps process emotional memory — the way emotionally charged experiences are encoded and recalled. Both sit close to the hippocampus, and both began losing tissue earlier in the disease process.

The researchers did not rely on MRI alone. They paired structural imaging with PET scans and digital pathology to examine whether tissue shrinkage tracked with tau, the protein strongly associated with Alzheimer’s. In the disease, tau can accumulate abnormally and form tangles that disrupt neuronal function. The study found that areas of grey matter shrinkage overlapped with tau-related changes, strengthening the case that these regions are not merely adjacent bystanders but part of the disease’s earliest visible footprint.

Grey matter — the part of the brain rich in nerve cell bodies — is essential for processing information. The authors argue that measuring tissue loss in the entorhinal cortex and amygdala together, rather than focusing mostly on the hippocampus, could improve early prediction of who is likely to develop cognitive decline.

Implications and Future Directions

If the findings hold up in further work, they could reshape the timing of Alzheimer’s care. Researchers envision a future in which doctors use these scans to stratify risk and identify people likely to develop cognitive decline soon, potentially even in their 30s or 40s for those with a family history or other risk factors. As Meaghan Morris, a neuropathologist and co-author, put it, better biomarkers could help identify who is likely to decline “in the near future” and allow treatment to begin earlier and earlier.

There is also a broader practical implication: as brain imaging becomes more accessible and less expensive, the threshold for using it as a screening or risk-assessment tool may fall. But the current work is still a research finding, not a ready-made clinical test. The study identifies candidate early biomarkers; it does not yet establish a validated diagnostic tool that can reliably predict Alzheimer’s years in advance for individual patients.

The next step, according to Miller, is to probe the molecular “neighbourhoods” around these tau-linked changes at a much smaller scale. Understanding the biology behind the atrophy could reveal new therapeutic targets — in other words, not just better ways to see the disease, but better ways to interrupt it.

Expert Perspectives

The study’s authors emphasise both urgency and caution. Miller described the disease as a “5-to-10-year play” in which the brain is changing long before memory symptoms appear, arguing that the value of the new findings lies in shifting intervention to a much earlier stage. Albert, meanwhile, underscored the underlying clinical aim: to treat people before symptoms begin, rather than after significant damage has already accumulated.

Those statements are encouraging, but they also highlight the gap between discovery and practice. The researchers themselves stop short of claiming that the new regions can yet be used in routine diagnosis. Instead, they present them as promising biomarkers that need further validation and mechanistic explanation before they can be translated into care.

The Bigger Picture

In neuroscience, Alzheimer’s research has increasingly moved from asking whether the disease is present to asking when and where its earliest detectable changes emerge. This study fits that shift. It also reflects a wider trend towards biomarker-based medicine: using measurable biological signals — imaging, proteins, pathology — to catch disease before symptoms become irreversible.

That matters far beyond Alzheimer’s. The central lesson is that complex brain disorders rarely announce themselves at the moment patients notice them. By the time memory fails, the disease may have been at work for years. Earlier detection, if reliable and ethically deployed, could change trial design, treatment timing and family planning. In a field where late diagnosis has long limited what medicine can do, even a modestly earlier window could make a meaningful difference.
